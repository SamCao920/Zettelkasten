## Summary
Unlike what is commonly to be understood, having greater information about consumers is actually beneficial for consumers. 
## Significance
This has significance for policy making, as policy could be designed to ensure that information is given to competing firms and improve consumer surplus.
## Scenario
The following are based in a situation where some consumers are “loyalists” (higher reserve price) and others indifferent between different products.
1. **No firm has any information:** this is the base case, where firms balance pricing discriminating loyalists and outpricing indifferent consumers. In doing so, they implement a calculated, random range of prices.
2. **One firms has information:** this firm is able to price discriminate its loyalists and outcompete other firms when it comes to the in different consumers.
3. **Firms receive private, imperfect information:** firms implicitly collude, making this the worse scenario for consumers. Since each firm believes that the other firms does not know what another customer is, they will charge a higher price than they would otherwise.
4. **All firms have access to public complete, symmetrical information:** firms price discriminate against loyalists, but price compete for indifferent consumers, driving the price down to marginal cost. In this case, the consumer is best-off since despite the loss of surplus from the price discrimination, the gains of surplus from the indifferent consumers outweighs that.
