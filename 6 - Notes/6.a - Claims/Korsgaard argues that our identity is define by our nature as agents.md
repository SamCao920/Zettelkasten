## Summary
Whereas Parfit deflates both the metaphysics and the normative importance of the conception of the self, Korsgaard argues that, from a practical standpoint, the second cannot be disregarded.
## Significance
She offers a counter-perspective to the argument that Parfit puts forth.
## Explanation
Korsgaard performs a partial Moorean shift. She argues that the metaphysics does not matter, and that only the normative implications are relevant. 
- In this way, she even accepts the more conservative and deflated version of the metaphysics of the self, that is Parfit's reductivism.
She argues that the normative claim must be preserve because if it is not, one would not be able to (or have the motivation to) plan for the future, and rational agency would be incoherent.
But she replaces this with a normative claim that we ought to only think of ourselves as only one thing (regardless of whether we actually are) since:
- We have only one body, and therefore, we have to collectively act.
- The agent is the source of the actions that they conduct.
A future person is only "you" because you were able to plan for them.
- A radical change in one's identity that does not originate in the agent disrupts one's identity, but this is not the case if one chooses to change themselves.
Additionally, collective entities can be people.

## Refutation
But Korsgaard's conception of the self has been challenged via empirical evidence.
- **Sperry and Gazzaniga split brain research**: they gave an instruction to one of the brain of a split brain subject, and after the action had been performed, and the other hemisphere of the brain is asked why they had did it, the other half of the brain would post hoc come up with a reason for their action.
The existence of confabulation undermines the assumption of people as rational and future-thinking agents, which means that it undermines the nature of a person defined as such.
