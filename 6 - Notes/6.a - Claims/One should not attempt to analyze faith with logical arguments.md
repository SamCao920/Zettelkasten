## Summary
One should not attempt to analyze a belief in a supreme power based on rigorous, logical argumentation.
## Significance
This is a viewpoint expressed by some when it comes to topics relating to the philosophy of religion.
## Examples
An example of this in literature is in *The Brothers Karamazov*, where Fyodor attempts to analyze his faithlessness with an argument about how there are no hooks in hell, since they don’t have furnaces to make iron and because there isn’t a ceiling in hell for the hooks to hang on. He seems to find a contradiction in whether hell has a ceiling or not.

> [!QUOTE] Fyodor Pavlovich, *The Brothers Karamazov*
> You see, however stupid I am about it, I keep thinking, I keep thinking — from time to time, of course, not all the while. It's impossible, I think, for the devils to forget to drag me down to hell with their hooks when I die. Then I wonder — hooks? Where would they get them? What of? Iron hooks? Where do they forge them? Have they a foundry there of some sort? The monks in the monastery probably believe that there's a ceiling in hell, for instance. Now I'm ready to believe in hell, but without a ceiling. It makes it more refined, more enlightened, more Lutheran that is. And, after all, what does it matter whether it has a ceiling or hasn't? But, do you know, there's a damnable question involved in it? If there's no ceiling there can be no hooks, and if there are no hooks it all breaks down, which is unlikely again, for then there would be none to drag me down to hell, and if they don't drag me down what justice is there in the world?

It seems that by attempting to justify something with argumentation, one can only trivialize it. Dostoevsky seems to make the case that this is not the right way to approach religion, this sort of materialist, trivially-rationalist approach.
