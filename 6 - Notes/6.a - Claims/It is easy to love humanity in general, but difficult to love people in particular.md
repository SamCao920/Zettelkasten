## Summary
This was an idea expressed by a speaker in one of Zossima’s stories. In essence, it argues that while it may be difficult to make grand propositions of one’s love of humanity in general (even being willing to sacrifice oneself for humanity), it seems much more difficult to actually love particular people, because the annoyance of mundane existence overpowers this more noble sense of universal love.
## Significance
This highlights a principle that is often neglected when we think about what it means to “love” humanity. The difficulty comes down to loving individual people, not the abstract idea of “humanity.”
## Quote

> [!QUOTE] Father Zossima, from *The Brothers Karamazov*
> "It's just the same story as a doctor once told me," observed the elder. "He was a man getting on in years, and undoubtedly clever. He spoke as frankiy as you, though in jest, in bitter jest. 'I love humanity,' he said, 'but I wonder at myself. **The more I love humanity in general, the less I love man in particular.** **In my dreams,**' he said, '**I have often come to making enthusiastic schemes for the service of humanity, and perhaps I might actually have faced crucifixion if it had been suddenly necessary; and yet I am incapable of living in the same room with any one for two days together, as I know by experience.** As soon as any one is near me, his personality disturbs my self- complacency and restricts my freedom. In twenty-four hours I begin to hate the best of men: one because he's too long over his dinner; another because he has a cold and keeps on blowing his nose. I become hostile to people the moment they come close to me. **But it has always happened that the more I detest men individually the more ardent becomes my love for humanity.**'"
## Explanation
This seems to connect to the overall idea in *The Brothers Karamazov* of the contrast between the base and the noble. It is an example of when the base (the annoyance with others in the mundanity of life) overpowers the noble (an idealistic desire to love humanity universally). 
Father Zossima tries to do both, taking responsibility for all of humanity’s sins while seeking to love them individually.
Ivan is almost like the man that is talked about in this passage. He has a penchant for viewing things through a spectator point of view, gaining enjoyment out of that, but when viewing things in particular, he begins to despise humanity. This is what drives his argument in “Rebellion.”
We have these romanticized notions about the character traits that humanity has with regards to the good (love, compassion, etc.), though this image breaks down when we view individuals in particular.
