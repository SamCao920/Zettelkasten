## Summary
There are different conceptions of what happiness entails, and Aristotle categorizes them into three major camps: those who think that pleasure is happiness, those who think that political success/honor is happiness, and those who value contemplation as happiness. Aristotle also states that it would be “grossly disconcerting” if it were the case that happiness was “random.” 
## Significance
Aristotle believes that true happiness can only be acquired through the final means: that is, contemplation. This forms the backbone of his philosophy.
## Explanation
Aristotle simple dismisses pure hedonism, presumably since it is something that does not involve “being an expert humans.” as pure hedonism is something that is achievable (at least in theory) by non-human organisms.
Aristotle rejects the definition of happiness as honor, since it is too “superficial.” “Honor is felt to depend more on those who confer than on him who receives it.” Perhaps honor is something that is too passive, and is not really an *activity* of the soul. Additionally, people honor others because they are good (virtuous). This means that goodness, and not honor, is the root of happiness.
- I don’t know if this point is the most convincing. It seems possible that one can still be happy with the praise, not one’s intrinsic virtue. 
Aristotle ultimately argues that the contemplative life is one that is able to produce happiness.
## Note
An important note to make clear here is that Aristotle’s conception of contemplation. His conception of “contemplation” is not one that promotes active discovery and curiosity towards new knowledge. Rather, it is reflecting on what one has already learned.
This is not to say that the Aristotelian contemplator does not seek out new knowledge, but that he does not constantly seek out new knowledge, since that is not what brings him happiness. It is rather the process of reflecting on knowledge that has been acquired that is the basis for happiness under this conception.
