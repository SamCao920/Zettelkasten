## Summary
An action is defined as being voluntary when the agent chooses to engage with the action, with this motivation originating internally. Additionally, even if the final actions are involuntary/compulsory, if it was voluntary action that lead to the involuntary/compulsory action, then the agent is still at fault.
## Significance
This lays out Aristotle’s definition for when an action is voluntary, which is the other side of the equation (the other side being when an action is compulsory) when determining moral responsibility.
## Explanation
There are two specific conditions required for an action to be considered voluntary:
- One is acting of one’s own volition.
- One understands the circumstances and particulars of the action that one wishes to engage in.
Given that, there are also two possible variations for the voluntary action.
1. You choose between chocolate ice cream and vanilla ice cream.
2. You choose to help a drowning child.
The difference between the two is that only the second is within the moral domain. Thus, only the second of the two actions can be considered praiseworthy or blameworthy.
## Note
Actions that are the result of desire or temper are considered voluntary, since our disposition is something that we can control, and our disposition is the reason for certain desires or tempers.
Additionally, moral responsibility tracks from motivation. For instance, licentiousness is more voluntary than cowardice since pain distracts the sufferers, and makes their actions less voluntary. In this way, the person who is more cowardly (in pain) is less responsible for the licentious person.
