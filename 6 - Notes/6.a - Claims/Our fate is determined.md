## Summary
The idea that our ultimate fates are ones that we do not get to determine. Rather, it is already determined, whether through from theological/divine process or a purely naturalistic one.
## Significance
This is a central idea in many faiths and belief system, and many works of art, so it is worth exploring.
## Examples
### Oedipus Rex
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=5&selection=51,18,53,1|I would be blind to misery not to pity my people kneeling at my feet.]]
	- This is a clever bit of irony that the play immediately throws at the audience, since at the end of the play, Oedipus is indeed blind, except he would be the one in misery, not his people.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=9&selection=14,0,20,38|He said thieves attacked them—a whole band, not single-handed, cut King Laius down. OEDIPUS: A thief, so daring, so wild, he'd kill a king?]]
	- Interesting how Oedipus mishears what Creon says and instead of referring to thieves, refers to a single, thief. It is as if he knows his fate, that he is that “thief” referred to.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=9&selection=57,0,59,17|Whoever killed the king may decide to kill me too, with the same violent hand—by avenging Laius I defend myself..]]
	- First of all, this does not come true, at least in *Oedipus Rex*. Regardless, it is ironic that the “violent hand” that eventually kills Laius’ killer is Oedipus, the same person who states that this would occur.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=12&selection=23,30,26,40|Whoever he is, a lone man unknown in his crime or one among many, let that man drag out his life in agony, step by painful step—]]
	- Oedipus not only makes reference to a single person to commits the crime, which is incongruous with the story told to him by Creon, he also predicts his future fate, where he is himself cast out of Thebes and forced to “drag out his life in agony, step by painful step–.”
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=12&selection=27,0,30,50|I curse myself as well . . . if by any chance he proves to be an intimate of our house, here at my hearth, with my full knowledge, may the curse I just called down on him strike me!]]
	- This line perhaps hits too close to home for Oedipus, as he will soon learn.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=12&selection=52,0,56,46|I hold the throne that he held then, possess his bed and a wife who shares our seed . . . why, our seed might be the same, children born of the same mother might have created blood-bonds between us if his hope of offspring had not met disaster—]]
	- Clearly, this is a prophecy Oedipus tells and which gets fulfilled by the end of the play.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=12&selection=58,0,58,48|So ,I will fight for him as if he were my father]]
	- Another bit of irony, as Laius is actually his *real* father.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=12&selection=66,0,67,37|Let no crops grow out of the earth for them— shrivel their women, kill their sons,]]
	- This is indeed something that occurs in Antigone, as the sons of Oedipus kill each other, and Antigone herself is killed by Creon. Only Ismene survives.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=13&selection=15,0,19,40|Laius was killed, they say, by certain travelers. OEDIPUS: I know—but no one can find the murderer.]]
	- Yet another instance of Oedipus (perhaps Freudian slipping) and stating that there was one murderer, instead of many.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=18&selection=12,0,14,40|Blind who now has eyes, beggar who now is rich, he will grope his way toward a foreign soil, a stick tapping before him step by step.]]
	- Tiresias predicts Oedipus’ future, and states it to him.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=23&selection=21,0,23,1|But reject a friend, a kinsman? I would as soon tear out the life within us, priceless life itself.]]
	- This is exactly what Creon does in Antigone, as he kills Antigone. Therefore, it is rather ironic that he is moralizing Oedipus about this now.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=25&selection=37,0,39,44|Then let him go, even if it does lead to my ruin, my death or my disgrace, driven from Thebes for life.]]
	- In a way, Creon is the one that eventually ruins Oedipus, since he did call Tiresias to meet with Oedipus, who reveals the nature of Oedipus’ relationship with his truth mother and father.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=28&selection=105,0,110,1|I heard all that and ran. I abandoned Corinth, from that day on I gauged its landfall only by the stars, running, always running toward some place where I would never see the shame of all those oracles come true.]]
	- Oedipus attempts to escape his fate, and not see it come true. Yet, it is simply the case that he cannot see the truth, not that he is particularly able to escape the truth.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=35&selection=25,0,34,39|MESSENGER: Polybus was nothing to you, that's why, not in blood. OEDIPUS: What are you saying—Polybus was not my father? MESSENGER: No more than I am. He and I are equals.]]
	- A chance meeting with a messenger (in fact, the very one that first brought the child Oedipus to Polybus) revealed the truth of Oedipus’ relationship with Polybus and Merope, showing the inevitability of fate.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=36&selection=48,0,55,14|MESSENGER: He called himself a servant of . . . if I remember rightly—Laius. JOCASTA turns sharply.]]
	- This seems to be the moment when Jocasta begins to piece things together, leading to her realization about Oedipus and her suicide.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=38&selection=26,0,26,40|but I, I count myself the son of Chance,]]
	- It is interesting how often Oedipus reference chance, here, he references himself as the child of chance. It seems that the very opposite is true, given the miraculous set of events that have occurred so far as to eventually lead Oedipus to find out the true nature of his birth.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=41&selection=13,0,17,15|My wife— she gave it to you?]]
	- This is probably the deciding moment for Oedipus that made him actually realize the truth.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=46&selection=46,0,50,20|Apollo, friends, Apollo— he ordained my agonies—these, my pains on pains! But the hand that struck my eyes was mine, mine alone—no one else— I did it all myself!]]
	- It seems that although our fates are the determined, the way that we may react to our fates is not. This complicates the debate regarding free will, and how we are able to act in a world that is predetermined, yet still have moral consequences and be responsible for our own actions.
	- Clearly, Oedipus has to be responsible for his own actions (that of continuing to search for the truth), yet the truth itself has already been determined.
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=47&selection=37,0,37,41|It's mine alone, my destiny—I am Oedipus!]]
- [[Oedipus Rex, Robert Fangles.pdf#page=49&selection=52,0,52,48|Well let my destiny come and take me on its way!]]
	- Oedipus accepts fate, at last (and with his eyes gone).
## Reference
- [[It is not always good to know the truth]]
	- It may well be the case that this endless pursuit of the truth is what ultimately determines our fate.
- [[The 'tragedy’ in tragic works of art often spring from the main character’s own actions]]
	- The fate that we are determined to, more often than not, could be one characterized by suffering (ex. Oedipus).
- [[The blind are able to see what the sighted are not]]
	- Our fate is determined often because we are not able to see its warning signs, or we don’t even realize that our fate is determined. This could be because we are able to “see” in the normal sense, while to be able to “see” our fate requires a different sense.
