# The Architecture of Agency: Self-Forming Acts, Character Development, and the Emergence of Moral Responsibility

To be morally responsible is to be the ultimate author of one's actions. Yet, for the libertarian, the very indeterminism required for such authorship threatens to reduce it to mere luck. This paper confronts this paradox directly, arguing that the architecture of responsible agency is not instantaneous but developmental, built over time through the crucible of self-forming choice.

Among the most sophisticated attempts to ground free will in a causally open universe is Robert Kane’s theory, which posits the existence of "Self-Forming Acts" (SFAs) as the bedrock of an agent's ultimate responsibility. These are rare, causally undetermined choices made during moments of intense internal conflict, and they serve as the crucial points at which we forge our own character. While powerful, this framework has faced significant criticism, particularly the charge that its reliance on indeterminism makes it vulnerable to the very problem of luck it seeks to solve.

I will argue that the Kanean framework, particularly when viewed through the developmental lens provided by John Lemos's defense, establishes a coherent and compelling model of human agency. By differentiating between the nascent agency of a child and the mature agency of an adult, this model demonstrates how full moral responsibility emerges and deepens over time through the cumulative effect of SFAs on an individual's character. This developmental perspective not only resolves key philosophical objections but also offers a more nuanced understanding of how we become the authors of our own moral lives. We begin by examining the foundational concepts of Kane’s theory.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. The Foundation of Libertarian Freedom: Kane's Theory of Self-Forming Acts

Within libertarian philosophy, Self-Forming Acts (SFAs) are of profound strategic importance. They are conceived as the source of an agent’s _underivative_ freedom—the ultimate wellspring from which the freedom of all other actions flows. Even actions that are causally determined by an agent's character can be considered free in a _derivative_ sense, provided that the character itself was shaped by prior, undetermined SFAs. These pivotal acts are the lynchpin of Robert Kane’s theory, grounding an agent's capacity for self-creation and, by extension, their moral responsibility.

An SFA, according to Kane, is a specific type of causally undetermined act with several defining characteristics:

- **Causal Indeterminism:** The outcome of an SFA is not determined by the agent's prior states, character, or any other preceding causes.
- **Profound Internal Conflict:** SFAs occur during difficult moments of choice, typically when an agent is torn between competing motivations, such as the pull of moral duty versus the allure of self-interest, or the desire for immediate gratification versus a long-term goal.
- **Dual Willings/Efforts:** In the moment of decision, the agent is simultaneously trying to bring about two or more incompatible outcomes. The agent actively wills, or puts effort into, each of the competing options.

To illustrate this concept, Kane presents the example of a businesswoman (BW) who, on her way to a career-defining meeting, witnesses a person being assaulted in an alley. She is torn: she has a strong desire to stop and help the victim (a moral motivation) and an equally strong desire to proceed to her meeting (a self-interested motivation). She cannot do both. In this moment of intense conflict, she engages in dual efforts: she simultaneously wills to stop and wills to continue on her way. Whichever decision she ultimately makes, the choice is causally undetermined. This act of resolving the conflict qualifies as an SFA.

Kane establishes a direct connection between these dual willings and the agent's responsibility. Because the agent actively wills to perform _both_ potential actions, she is responsible for the outcome, whichever one it may be. In the case of the businesswoman, if she stops to help, she is responsible for that choice because it was a direct consequence of her willing to do so. Had she proceeded to her meeting, she would have been equally responsible for that action for the same reason. This structure is Kane's primary tool for solving the significant philosophical problems that arise from introducing indeterminism into the chain of causation, most notably the problem of luck.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. The Conundrum of Indeterminism: The Luck Objection and the Clarke/Mele Challenge

The most persistent and formidable criticism leveled against libertarianism is the "luck objection." In its simplest form, the objection states that if an action is causally undetermined, its occurrence appears to be a matter of chance or luck rather than a controlled exercise of agency. If an agent's choice is not determined by their character, beliefs, and desires, then it seems to be a random event for which they cannot be held morally responsible.

This objection is often sharpened through a thought experiment involving a "counterpart agent." Imagine the businesswoman (BW) in a world where she decides to stop and help the assault victim. If her choice is truly undetermined, then there exists a possible world with an identical past and identical laws of nature where her counterpart agent—a person with the exact same character, memories, and motivations up to the moment of choice—decides instead to go on to her meeting. As Alfred Mele argues, if "there is nothing about the agent’s powers, capacities, states of mind, moral character, and the like that explains this difference in outcome," then the choice seems reducible to mere luck.

Kane proposes to solve the luck problem by appealing to the concept of dual willings, supported by an argument from analogy. He asks us to imagine an assassin who attempts to shoot a prime minister. The assassin pulls the trigger, but due to some indeterminacy in his nervous system, the path of the bullet is not causally determined. The bullet hits its target, and the prime minister is killed. Kane argues that despite the indeterminism, the assassin is fully responsible for the killing because he _willed_ the outcome. He then analogizes this to the businesswoman: although her decision to stop was undetermined, she is responsible because it was a consequence of her willing it.

It is at this point that Randolph Clarke and Alfred Mele mount a powerful objection that targets the central flaw in this analogy. The "Clarke/Mele Objection" proceeds as follows:

1. We are only willing to hold the assassin responsible for the killing because we instinctively presume that he _**freely willed**_ to kill the prime minister in the first place.
2. For the analogy to successfully transfer responsibility to the businesswoman, we must likewise presume that she _**freely willed**_ both of her competing options (to stop and help; to go to her meeting).
3. This forces Kane to account for the freedom of these initial willings. He has suggested that these willings can be considered free if they satisfy a good set of _compatibilist_ criteria (e.g., they are not coerced or compelled and align with her wishes).
4. As Mele points out, this move appears to be deeply problematic for a libertarian. If meeting compatibilist criteria is sufficient to establish the freedom of the willings that precede an SFA, why is causal indeterminism necessary for freedom at all? This appeal seems to undermine the entire motivation for the libertarian project.

The Clarke/Mele objection thus frames a critical dilemma for the Kanean view, presenting a seemingly unanswerable problem of internal inconsistency. It is this challenge that necessitates a more nuanced, developmental defense of the theory.

### 3. A Developmental Defense: Differentiating Adult and Childhood Agency

The core of a robust defense against the Clarke/Mele objection lies in a critical, developmental distinction between the agency of a mature adult and that of a young child. This differentiation is not an ad-hoc maneuver but a necessary clarification for understanding how full, libertarian moral responsibility can be grounded without collapsing into inconsistency. By examining the nature of Self-Forming Acts (SFAs) at different stages of life, we can see how compatibilist freedom serves as a necessary foundation for the eventual emergence of a deeper, incompatibilist freedom.

#### Adult SFAs: The Weight of a Self-Formed Character

An adult, like the businesswoman (BW) in Kane's example, is considered fully responsible for her SFA precisely because her agency is historically conditioned. Her dual willings—to act morally and to act out of self-interest—are not arbitrary impulses but expressions of a developed character.

- On the Kanean view, this character has been shaped by a long history of prior, causally undetermined SFAs. Each past SFA contributed to the person she has become, making her the principal author of her own motivational structure.
- Therefore, when her willings emerge from this character in a manner that satisfies compatibilist criteria (i.e., they are not coerced or compelled), they are indeed free in a compatibilist sense. However, they also possess a **derivative libertarian freedom**—that is, a freedom that derives its libertarian quality not from the immediate choice itself, but from the fact that the character from which it issues is a product of past, underivatively free choices. Her willings are free not _just_ because they meet compatibilist standards, but because they flow from a self she is ultimately responsible for creating.

#### Childhood's First SFAs: Minimal Agency and External Origins

In stark contrast, a young child facing their first SFA bears very little moral responsibility for the choice. The reasons for this are fundamental to the developmental account:

- The child’s conflicting desires are products of external factors—namely, genetics and environment. They have not yet had the opportunity to perform the causally undetermined choices necessary to shape their own character.
- In these initial SFAs, the dual willings that precede the undetermined choice meet _only_ the compatibilist criteria of freedom. The desires are "hers" in the sense that they are not the product of external coercion or internal compulsion, but they lack the deeper grounding in a self-authored character.
- Because the child has made a minimal contribution to the character that gives rise to the dilemma, her responsibility for the resulting SFA is correspondingly minimal.

This distinction directly resolves the inconsistency identified by Mele. Meeting compatibilist criteria for freedom is a necessary but **insufficient** condition for the _full_ moral responsibility characteristic of mature agency. Full responsibility is a richer, more demanding status that requires a history of character-forming, undetermined choices. The compatibilist freedom of a child's first willings serves as the entry point into a process of self-formation, not its final destination. This analysis, however, raises a further question: how does an agent dynamically transition from the minimal responsibility of childhood to the full responsibility of adulthood?

### 4. The Genesis and Growth of Moral Responsibility

To fully appreciate the Kanean model, it is crucial to trace the _process_ by which an individual transforms from an agent with minimal responsibility to one who bears the full weight of their choices. This developmental arc is not instantaneous but occurs through the cumulative impact of Self-Forming Acts (SFAs) on character formation over a lifetime. Each SFA, beginning in childhood, serves as a building block in the architecture of a moral self.

#### The Source of Minimal Responsibility: The Case of a Child's First SFA

Consider the example of a young child, Rosa, who has been told not to take a cookie but feels a strong desire to sneak one. This internal conflict—between obeying her parents and satisfying her desire—presents the occasion for her first SFA. She simultaneously wills to sneak the cookie and to obey. Suppose she makes the undetermined choice to take the cookie. Although her character is largely a product of genetics and environment, she still bears a _minimal_ degree of responsibility for this choice. This responsibility is grounded in two key factors:

1. **Causal Contribution:** Rosa’s sustained effort is a necessary causal factor in the action's occurrence. While the _outcome_ is undetermined, her effort is a determinative factor in ensuring that a choice is made at all. As the Kanean defense notes, "had she not exerted the effort...then the choice to do so would never be made." In this way, Rosa herself plays a significant causal role in determining which of the two possible outcomes is actualized. Her effort is not an external force but an expression of her own agency, meeting compatibilist "absence of constraint" conditions.
2. **Act of Endorsement:** In making the undetermined decision, Rosa endorses one aspect of her pre-formed character (the desire for the cookie) over another (the desire to obey). While she did not create these competing desires, she _did_ determine which one would govern her behavior in that moment. This act of endorsement is a foundational moment of self-determination, marking her first contribution to the authorship of her character.

#### The Cumulative Mechanism of Character Formation

Each SFA, however minimally responsible, contributes to the shaping of an agent's character over time. With each successive undetermined choice, the agent's character becomes incrementally less a product of external factors and more a product of their own self-forming decisions. This cumulative process is the central mechanism of the developmental theory, embodying Kane's insight that **"incompatibilist freedom and control presupposes compatibilist freedom and control."** We cannot achieve libertarian freedom "in one fell swoop"; we must get there "step wise," by exercising compatibilist control over our choices. Rosa's first, minimally responsible SFA is the first necessary "step" on this path. Her choice to take the cookie makes it slightly more likely that she will prioritize immediate desire over obedience in the future. Over years, a long series of such SFAs solidifies tendencies and values, creating a character that is increasingly self-made.

This process explains why an adult's responsibility is magnified. When the businesswoman (BW) faces her dilemma, she is responsible on a deeper level than Rosa. She is responsible not only for the specific undetermined choice she makes but also for having cultivated the very character that gives rise to the dilemmatic situation. In contrast, the dilemma Rosa faces is **"thrust upon them by genetic and environmental factors which are beyond their control."** The robust, incompatibilist freedom of an adult is not created in a vacuum; it is built upon a developmental foundation of compatibilist freedom, which allows for the initial choices that empower an agent to gradually become the ultimate source of their own actions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5. Conclusion

The formidable challenge posed by the Clarke/Mele objection—that Robert Kane's libertarianism appears to rely on the very compatibilist criteria it seeks to transcend—forces a deeper and more nuanced exploration of his theory. The resolution, as I have argued, lies not in abandoning the theory's core tenets but in viewing them through a developmental lens. By tracing the architecture of agency from its nascent stages in childhood to its mature form in adulthood, a coherent and powerful account of moral responsibility emerges.

The core of this defense rests on a crucial distinction. The minimal responsibility a child bears for their first Self-Forming Acts (SFAs) is grounded in compatibilist freedom; their willings are their own, free from coercion, but issue from a character they did not create. This initial, compatibilist-grounded agency is the necessary first step for a lifelong process of self-formation. In contrast, the full responsibility of an adult is grounded in a richer, libertarian freedom. An adult's willings issue from a character they have forged through a history of their own undetermined choices, granting them a derivative libertarian freedom that a child lacks.

This developmental perspective successfully demonstrates that causally undetermined SFAs remain essential for "ultimate responsibility." As Kane notes, incompatibilist freedom is built step-wise upon a foundation of compatibilist freedom. Without these pivotal, undetermined choices, "our characters would then be all too much a product of genetic and environmental factors and not authored by ourselves." SFAs are the very mechanism by which individuals transform from beings shaped by their circumstances into agents who are the primary authors of their own character. It is through these acts that we gradually construct our moral selves and, in doing so, earn the profound status of being responsible for who we are and what we do.
