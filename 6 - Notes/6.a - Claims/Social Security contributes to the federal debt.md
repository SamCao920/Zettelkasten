## Summary
The Social Security program, unlike what some claim, does indeed contribute to the overall federal debt, as since 2010, it has run a deficit, meaning that the amount it receives in payroll taxes is less than the amount that the program spends on benefits.
## Significance
This demonstrates the great necessity for reform in the Social Security system, as over the next decade, Social Security could contribute 4.1 trillion to the overall federal debt.

## Explaination
Often, it is explained that Social Security has not contributed to the debt because they ran surplus in the past, the products of which is stored as the Social Security Trust Fund. The idea is that Social Security could dip into these funds when they run deficits, and thus, not add to the deficit. However, view is facile for several reasons:
1. The trust fund is expected to run out by the early 2030s.
2. The trust fund is not actually consisting of liquid assets. Rather, it is made up of special-issue Treasury bonds, which means that when Social Security needs to withdraw some amount from the trust fund, the federal government actually needs to find the money to for the trust fund through either tax hikes or increases to the federal deficit.
