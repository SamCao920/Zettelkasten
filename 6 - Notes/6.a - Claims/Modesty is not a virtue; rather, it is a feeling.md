## Summary
Aristotle says that modesty is not a virtue. Rather, it is a feeling, something that is readily mutable, rather than a disposition or a state. He defines it as the “fear of disrepute.”
## Significance
Normally, modesty is defined as a virtue. Aristotle, however, defines it a feeling. He also has some very specific thoughts regarding the nature of modesty, viz. who it is good for.
## Explanation
Aristotle argues that modesty is good for young people, but bad for virtuous adults.
Regarding virtuous adults, he makes the argument that:
1. Virtuous people does not do shameful things.
2. You only feel modesty (shame, as defined by Aristotle) when you are shameful.
3. $\therefore$ Virtuous people do not feel modesty.
Virtuous people only “feel” modesty “in hypothesis,” in other words, they can imagine themselves feeling shame if they were to do something not virtuous.
In young people, however, since they live by “passion” rather than “reason,” they are prone to making mistakes due to their inexperience. Then, having the modesty to admit their mistakes and change themselves becomes a boon to their development.
Modesty is better than shamelessness, but still not as good as not being modest (for the adult).
