## Introduction

The most transformative drivers of human innovation and development are general-purpose technologies (GPTs). Throughout history, GPTs such as steam, electricity, and computers have led to periods of rapid economic growth and social restructuring [@bresnahanGeneralPurposeTechnologies1995, p. 0]. In the past decade, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has been heralded as the next GPT. Yet AI possesses unique characteristics compared to its predecessors, leading many to argue that it could reshape the world even more radically than earlier innovations. I contend that while AI shares the broad applicability and transformational promise of past GPTs, its adoption will be markedly faster and more uneven due to its digital nature, near-zero marginal costs, and generality. Its economic effects will be more rapid, disruptive, and polarizing—demanding proactive institutional adaptation to prevent rising inequality and market concentration.

## Context & Analytical Framework

First, a brief history of the GPTs considered in this essay. One of the first GPTs of the modern era was steam. Developed in England in the late 18th century, steam had a negligible impact on overall productivity and economic growth, being limited to a few industries prior to the 1830s. It was only in the mid-19th century, when high-power steam engines and complementary technologies such as railways and steamships were further improved, that a surge in productivity occurred. From 1870 to 1910, steam accounted for approximately 29% of productivity growth in the UK [@craftsArtificialIntelligenceGeneralpurpose2021, p. 524]. Electricity followed a similar pattern. Despite its commercial viability in the late 19th century, the technology did not have a prominent effect on productivity until around a decade after a “user-friendly” breakthrough, in the 1920s–1930s in the US, when ~23% of firms were using electric power and electricity contributed around 10% of overall productivity growth [@filippucciMacroeconomicProductivityGains2025, p. 16–17]. Most nations only fully began adopting electricity after World War II. By contrast, the adoption of semiconductor-based computing occurred much faster than previous technologies. Within little more than a decade from its initial commercial viability in the late 1970s and 1980s, US labor productivity growth rose from ~1.6% per year (1974–1995) to over 3% (1995–2004) [@craftsArtificialIntelligenceGeneralpurpose2021, p. 525]. Merely ten years after the personal computer’s introduction, ~40% of US firms were using computers [@filippucciMacroeconomicProductivityGains2025, p. 16]. I use these timelines to benchmark AI’s diffusion rate in §Adoption Speed.

Now, let us define some characteristics that all GPTs share. A GPT is a single, generic technology that is pervasive, has potential for further technological improvements, increases the profitability of additional innovation, and has many uses with broad spillover effects [@craftsArtificialIntelligenceGeneralpurpose2021, p. 521]. Commonly, GPTs follow an S-curve style of adoption: initially the technology struggles with diffusion since firms are reluctant to upend their existing operations, but as it improves and prior investments mature, adoption accelerates before eventually plateauing. A key driver of this process is continual improvement of the GPT over time, which increases the incentive for firms to adopt [@bresnahanGeneralPurposeTechnologies1995, p. 5]. Alongside technological development, GPTs often create complementary innovations. For example, the invention of electricity led to the creation of light bulbs, batteries, and eventually electric vehicles. In this way, GPTs produce a wealth of entrepreneurial opportunities both directly related to the GPT and in adjacent industries.

Some negative similarities also persist between AI and past GPTs. For example, GPTs tend to increase the share of output allocated to capital owners, thereby raising inequality. This occurred during the British Industrial Revolution, when the share of profits in GDP allocated to capital owners rose from 17.2% in 1770 to 29.5% in 1860 [@craftsArtificialIntelligenceGeneralpurpose2021, p. 526–528]. Additionally, GPT development can occur unevenly across the world: in 2023, 95% of global AI research was carried out by the US and China [@davidComputerDynamoModern1989, p. 18–19n11].

At the same time, there are also pronounced differences between GPTs, which we can measure using the following metrics: adoption speed and diffusion channels, complementary assets and infrastructure, impact on productivity, impact on labor, impact on market structure, and impact on regulation and society. These are the metrics by which we are examining AI compared to previous GPTs.

## AI vs. Previous GPTs: Speed and Economic Impact

### Adoption Speed

One of the most apparent differences between AI and previous GPTs is their adoption speed. Steam and electricity especially required capital-heavy investments. Steam, in its most common forms—the steamboat and steam train—demanded extensive investment in boats, locomotives, and rail. Likewise, electricity required construction of power plants and transmission grids. Computers had much lower initial costs, though their adoption still necessitated major changes to organizational processes, software systems, and data architectures. By contrast, the adoption of AI is faster since delivery is largely digital. From 2024 to 2025, AI adoption in the US rose from 4% to 6%; if this trend continues, AI is predicted to reach ~50–60% adoption within the decade [@filippucciMacroeconomicProductivityGains2025, p. 17].

Several factors explain this rapid rate of adoption. First, distribution is digital and low-marginal-cost: firms can call APIs or deploy managed services without owning heavy infrastructure [@erdilExplosiveGrowthAI2023, p. 9; @brynjolfssonArtificialIntelligenceModern2017, p. 21]. In the past two years, quality-adjusted AI models fell in cost by over 80% [@andreDevelopmentsArtificialIntelligence2025, p. 8]. Second, open-source frameworks and model weights further lower entry barriers, widening participation beyond large incumbents. This counteracts the expected concentration of power in the hands of first innovators typical of earlier GPTs. Third, the learning curve is shallow: natural-language interfaces and copilots let non-experts operate capable tools, with especially large gains for less-experienced users [@filippucciMacroeconomicProductivityGains2025, p. 17]. AI’s constraints differ from earlier GPTs. Rather than extensive hardware, diffusion depends on intangible complements—data governance and quality, deployment and monitoring, and workforce capability. For many firms, the binding constraint is not model access but clean data and redesigned workflows [@brynjolfssonArtificialIntelligenceModern2017, p. 4–10].

As a result, while AI is being adopted quickly by many companies, the intensive process of embedding it into core operations is slower and varies significantly across organizations. Consequently, AI is still likely to exhibit a productivity J-curve: early investments in complements and learning depress measured gains before benefits arrive, as with prior GPTs, but the trough should be shallower and shorter because less investment in physical infrastructure is required.

### Economic Impacts

Another major difference between AI and past GPTs is the extent of its impact on labor. Both steam and electricity and their complementary tools displaced manual labor while expanding industrial employment [@agrawalWeWantLess2023, p. 155; @soroushianWhatWavesAutomation2024]. In this way, labor productivity rose with adoption, leading to rising wages over time [@davidComputerDynamoModern1989, p. 14–15]. Computers automated routine cognitive tasks such as bookkeeping, data entry, and basic calculations. AI will be able to automate an even larger share of tasks, as it can perform some non-routine cognitive functions [@agrawalWeWantLess2023, p. 156].

This has several implications. Many professional tasks that rely on analysis, reasoning, and creative problem-solving will be automated by AI, increasing productivity and thus generating economic growth [@bresnahanGeneralPurposeTechnologies1995, p. 1–2]. At the same time, new tasks are created around AI integration, including prompting, oversight, evaluation, and data stewardship [@craftsArtificialIntelligenceGeneralpurpose2021, p. 526]. For the individual worker, there are two possible, non-exclusive outcomes. First, due to AI’s shallow learning curve, labor productivity may be boosted broadly, reducing inequality [@agrawalWeWantLess2023, p. 155]. Second, because of its skill-amplifying nature, already productive workers and firms may become even more productive, widening the skill gap [@brynjolfssonArtificialIntelligenceModern2017, p. 8–9]. Which of the two effects takes hold will depend on institutional factors like education systems, labor market policies, and access to AI technologies. What is certain is AI’s observed effect on individual productivity, with generative models producing a 30–60% productivity gain depending on the task [@filippucciMacroeconomicProductivityGains2025, p. 9].

Although AI can perform a wider range of tasks than previous GPTs, it is likely to cause long-term job displacement rather than permanent job loss. Historically, GPTs have primarily caused reallocation across sectors, not outright unemployment. One thing is certain: as past GPTs have done, AI will create new tasks [@soroushianWhatWavesAutomation2024; @brynjolfssonArtificialIntelligenceModern2017, p. 20]. Another unique characteristic of AI is its ability to enhance itself and increase the productivity of knowledge creation [@craftsArtificialIntelligenceGeneralpurpose2021, p. 522]. This transforms AI from a static capital input into a dynamic, self-improving asset. By recursively optimizing its own production processes, AI could generate compounding returns on innovation, shortening the time between productivity breakthroughs. Though highly uncertain, the result could be a structural shift in the economy's growth trajectory, with shorter J-curves and higher equilibrium productivity growth. This recursive innovation mechanism could even result in an “economic singularity,” whereby technological progress becomes self-reinforcing, driving productivity growth beyond historical GPT patterns [@nordhausAreWeApproaching2021, p. 305–315].

A primary influence on the pace and economic impact of AI development is regulation. In past GPTs, safety and competition regulations evolved alongside technology. In the UK, the Factory Acts were passed to protect workers using steam engines in factories; likewise, in the US, antitrust statutes prevented monopolies in electricity from stifling competition [@1833FactoryAct; @ShermanAntiTrustAct2021]. AI is likely to be subject to similar regulation, perhaps even more extensive [@erdilExplosiveGrowthAI2023, p. 9–10]. Regulation’s effect is nuanced: clear, risk-based rules can accelerate adoption by building trust and interoperability, while vague or heavy ex-ante requirements, uncertain copyright and privacy rules, or data-localization mandates can slow diffusion and entrench incumbents. The economic trajectory of AI thus hinges critically on institutional adaptation—particularly regulatory design—to balance productivity benefits against risks of polarization and market concentration.

## Conclusion

AI satisfies the criteria of a GPT yet differs in tempo: digital delivery accelerates diffusion at the extensive margin, while deeper integration proceeds more slowly, producing a comparatively shallow productivity J-curve. Its effects on labor, productivity, and market structure will be significant, amplified by its recursive capacity to accelerate innovation itself. The gains will be uneven, mediated by differences in data quality, compute access, and institutional complements. History shows that GPTs can widen inequality and entrench incumbents if unchecked, but also that sound policy can steer them toward inclusive growth. To ensure AI delivers an augmentation dividend rather than an automation trap, policymakers must invest in human capital, broaden access, and enforce pro-competition regulation. With such measures, AI’s rapid adoption can translate into broad-based and durable productivity growth.
