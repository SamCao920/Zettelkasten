In the 18 years following the publication of _Bad Samaritans_, many of economist Ha-Joon Chang’s hypotheses have been put to the test, including his views on patent and intellectual property rights (IPRs) regulation. I will seek to elucidate the veracity of Chang’s claims, made in chapter 6 of his seminal work, _Bad Samaritans_, “Windows 98 in 1997.” Since 2007, evidence has largely vindicated Chang: patents can spur R&D in high–fixed-cost sectors, but rigid IPR regulation via diluted patent content and interlocking patents raises entry barriers, slows diffusion, and entrenches incumbents.

In the past 18 years, one thing has remained constant: patents and IPR still exist. And with them remains the fundamental justification for their existence, namely that IPRs incentivize innovation.

Now, why would IPRs incentivize innovation? It seems that the very nature of IPRs (especially their exclusive, monopolistic nature) would _prohibit_, not encourage, innovation. To understand this, we must understand the concept of a “natural monopoly.” [[A natural monopoly is produced when fixed costs are high and variable costs are low in an industry|A natural monopoly arises when the fixed costs are high in an industry, but the marginal costs are low. This means that once production, which may be initially expensive, starts, the firm can quickly achieve an economy of scale.]] The key component to consider is the high initial costs of production, which necessitate that few are able to meet the high capital requirement. Those who are able to afford such capital requirements thereby take on immense risk, which demands that the reward be adequately commensurate. Here is where patents and IPRs come in. As monopolies provide their holders with disproportionate surplus, they can be utilized as a form of reward for the risk-takers, without whom innovations in science and technology would not have occurred. If there had been no legal protection of the result of such a high capital investment in the form of protections for IPRs, competitors could create imitations, thereby bringing the price—and thus profit—down for the original capital owner.

Herein lies the nucleus of Chang’s contention: the government controls the length of a patent and, thereby, the size of the carrot which leads people to take substantial risks.

But how big, _exactly_, ought the carrot be?

Chang argued that the world’s governments have provided too generous a benefit for capital owners, which, paradoxically, leads to the originally unparadoxical conclusion: that such patents and IPRs create a barrier to innovation and benefit only incumbents in the market. But is this the case? Let us examine his argument:

Returning to the discussion regarding the fundamental justification for patents and natural monopolies, Chang puts forth a challenge, arguing that [[Material incentives are not the only thing that motivates people to produce new ideas|material incentives are not the only thing that motivates people to produce new ideas]]. He points to the amount of research conducted by non-profit and government organizations, which seems to be testament to the idea that research need not be conducted by profit-seeking private actors.[@changBadSamaritansMyth2008, p. 124-125] However, there are a few inaccuracies in such an argument. First, from 1996–2016, 67 to 97 percent of drug development was conducted by the private sector.[@chakravarthyPublicPrivateSectorContributions2016, p. 2] This seems to undermine the claim that non-material incentives play a substantial role in funding investment in new research. Yes, perhaps some research into pharmaceuticals was conducted via government funding or through private charities, but the contribution of funding through such sources pales in comparison with private, for-profit funding. In addition, much of the government research focuses on “basic science,” i.e., research that, although crucial, does not directly contribute to the development of pharmaceuticals. Rather, it is primarily private institutions that carry out the expensive, risky, and highly specialized process of creating actual medicines.[@chakravarthyPublicPrivateSectorContributions2016, p. 5, 10]

Chang also makes the claim that patents are not needed in most industries, as often new inventions are sufficiently difficult to imitate to provide the innovator with a substantive first-mover advantage.[@changBadSamaritansMyth2008, p.125-126] Regarding this, Chang seems to be mistaken: the pharmaceutical industry is an example of an industry where it is extremely difficult to make imitations of competitors’ products.[@kirchhoffBiosimilarsKeyRegulatory2017] Yet, around 65 percent of commercial inventions in the pharmaceutical industry would not have existed had patents not been granted.[@cockburnImportancePatentsInnovation2015, p.740] That being said, Chang’s claim is indeed applicable in some industries; for example, the fashion industry has achieved what some term a “low-IP equilibrium,” where rampant copying actually drives innovation.[@raustialaPiracyParadoxInnovation2006, p. 1732-1734] In these industries, the existence of patents would only impede progress.

Industries that require patents and IPRs face a further problem, however: new patents are increasingly trivial, even potentially malicious, which Chang attributes to [[Inventors have an incentive to lower the bar of originality for patents and IPRs|inventors’ incentive to lower the bar of originality for patents and IPRs]]. First, he points out, there is theft of traditional knowledge from developing nations; knowledge that, despite being widely known in its area of origin, has been patented by other countries.[@changBadSamaritansMyth2008, p.137] Second, he identifies the phenomenon of “[[Interlocking patents is a barrier to innovation|interlocking patents]]”: patents have tended to be increasingly fragmented and minute, meaning that any new major innovation would have to navigate a landscape of dozens, perhaps hundreds, of other relevant patents [@changBadSamaritansMyth2008, p.139-140]. Regarding his former concern, systems instituted since the publication of his work have now placed much stricter limits on the ability of nations to patent traditional knowledge. For example, the 2025 WIPO Treaty on Intellectual Property, Genetic Resources and Associated Traditional Knowledge (GRATK) will “enhance the efficacy, transparency and quality of the patent system with regard to genetic resources and traditional knowledge…, and prevent patents from being granted erroneously for inventions that are not novel…”[@alvesWIPOsNewTreaty2024] In addition, many countries have implemented laws or compiled traditional knowledge databases to further prevent theft of traditional knowledge.[@gutierrezHowLatinAmerica2020; @canadianintellectualpropertyofficePatentsBasedGenetic2025] Regarding the latter concern, while it is very much extant, there are no “easy” potential solutions to implement. “Patent pools,” where all members of an industry cross-license all relevant patents to one another, are difficult to coordinate, especially without government intervention. Yet, excessive government intervention in promoting patent pools could, once more, stifle innovation. The problem of interlocking patents identified by Chang is a complex one and will require nuanced regulation and institutional reform in order to mitigate it, being beyond the scope of this short essay.

Ultimately, Chang claims that a strict and overregulated patent and IPR regime (in the manners previously described) hurts developing countries.[@changBadSamaritansMyth2008, p.141] The high cost of running a patent office, especially when having to comply with variegated international regulations, acts as [[International IPR and patent laws benefit incumbents|a drag on the economies of developing, less technologically advanced nations]]. International regulations represent a misallocation of resources that prevent less affluent countries from investing in crucial aspects of government functions, such as education, health care, or law enforcement. Moreover, they prevent developing nations from being able to acquire new knowledge in the long term, thereby deterring these nations from further economic development. Recent evidence bears this out. Implementing international IPR regulation has indeed imposed measurable administrative and fiscal burdens on low-income states in the form of fixed outlays to stand up offices, recurrent enforcement costs, and royalty payments.[@maskusIntellectualPropertyChallenges2001, p. 466-470] Moreover, comparative reviews find that without strong local absorptive capacity, regulation chiefly advantages incumbents, raising prices and constraining economic growth rather than benefiting developing nations as a whole.[@hassanIntellectualPropertyDeveloping, p.27-30]

Chang’s conclusion that IPR should be designed to help developing countries be more productive and attain new technology is a cogent one. Thus, while the “carrot” of monopoly remains defended as necessary for innovation, and some empirical data remain contrary to Chang’s claims, the overall evidence leans in favor of Chang’s conclusion: as global inequality increases, it is up to our international organizations to recognize the faults in their systems, rectify them, and promote a more equitable and inclusive global community.
