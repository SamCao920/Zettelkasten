# Should Meritocracy Be Considered a Fair System that Rewards Talent and Effort, or a Myth that Disguises Structural Inequality?
The question is centred around two key concepts, that of meritocracy and that of structural inequality. Thus, in the first instance, any response will depend on the definition for each of these key concepts. Definitions of such socially embedded concepts are never context-independent. Thus, as a second step, it is necessary to decide on (in an informed manner) relevant scenarios to be considered when approaching the question. Finally, a question to consider is how and why the sample meritocracy is implemented as well as whether it is temporary or not.
## Introduction
In contemporary discussions of meritocracy, two perspectives are common. Either meritocracy is the champion of individual abilities and effort, or it is a sham, a societal misconception which only entrenches existing power and wealth differentials. I argue that this is a false binary: meritocracy can be both, *at difference times.* This argument is developed within the context of liberal societies—the only context in which meritocracy is seriously advanced as a fairness claim, %%since it presupposes commitments to individual liberty and family autonomy that liberalism independently affirms%%.[^1] Through a diachronic analysis, we will find that meritocracy is either illiberal, or fundamentally an incoherent, self-defeating concept. 
In this paper, I specifically attack desert-based meritocracy, the principle that the allocation of social goods is intrinsically justified because individuals deserve outcomes proportional to their merits. [@mulliganMeritocracy2023, sec. 2.2] I concede that instrumental merit, i.e., it is clear that allowing the most qualified to hold a position is a sound principle, regardless of how one is able to become the most qualified, is valid to a certain degree.[^2]
## Setup: what Meritocracy Requires to Be Justified
Desert-based meritocracy (DBM) claims to be a *just* system of allocation where positions and rewards go to those who earn them through relevant talent and effort, disregarding irrelevant characteristics.[@fishkinJusticeEqualOpportunity, p. 22] But what does it mean to be just? We can distinguish between two interpretations.[@fishkinJusticeEqualOpportunity, p. 22] 
	(1) *Procedural justice* states that if the procedure involved in the distribution of social goods is justice, then the outcome of that distribution is just. John Rawls’ terms this “pure procedural justice,” giving the analogy of a fair gambling game, where as long as the procedure is carried out according to fair rules, the outcome is just—though it may result in a differential distribution of goods. [@rawlsTheoryJusticeRevised1999, p. 74-76] 
	(2) *Background justice* regards the circumstances which must hold for procedural justice to be meaningful, that is, that preexisting institutions must be fair before the procedure executes, otherwise the procedure just launders whatever inequalities preceded it. 
It seems that any strong meaning of “justice” must satisfy the criteria from both interpretations of the term. Disregard the former, and there is no guarantee that the the people who are the hardest working are compensated for their efforts. Disregard the latter, and the society merely entrenches extant inequities over time. 
Therefore, to be a just system of distribution, meritocracy must satisfy justice in this strong sense. 
Let’s consider (2) in more specificity. Following the convention first explicated in Rawls’s *A Theory of Justice*, we can distinguish between two why in which background justice may be violated: natural contingencies and social contingencies.[@rawlsTheoryJusticeRevised1999, sec. 12] Natural contingencies represent differences in ability which are innate, broadly encompassing traits such as strength, intelligence, and creativity. Social contingencies represent differences in ability which are due to social circumstances outside one’s control, such as the one’s family wealth, class background, and level/quality of schooling.
Background justice, in the context of DBM, therefore requires mitigating contingencies that are arbitrary from the perspective of merit, that is, contingencies which bear no relationship to the talent and effort M claims to reward. Social contingencies satisfy this criterion straightforwardly: family wealth or class background has no bearing on one's merits, and permitting them to shape outcomes simply launders pre-existing inequality through a meritocratic procedure. Natural contingencies, by contrast, are precisely what DBM takes itself to be tracking. Talent and effort are themselves natural endowments, and to require their equalization would dissolve M entirely, since there would be nothing left to reward. This is not merely a stipulation: it reflects the meritocrat's own preferred account, shared by Mulligan and Miller alike, that natural talent is the legitimate desert base and social circumstances are the distorting factor.[@millerTwoCheersMeritocracy1996; @mulliganJusticeMeritocraticState2017, sec. 4.3]
Background justice for DBM therefore requires equalization of social contingencies specifically; call this Equality of Life Chances (ELC), following Fishkin.[@fishkinJusticeEqualOpportunity, p. 32-34] Without ELC, M does not instantiate background justice and therefore cannot satisfy the strong sense of justice identified above, becoming a fair procedure running on an unfair background, producing outcomes that appear deserved but merely reflect products of the unequal background distribution. 
In short, M is normatively justified if and only if ELC holds.
## The Liberal Commitment that Creates the Problem
Liberalism generally upholds the principle of Family Autonomy (FA): that parents the right to invest time, attention, culture, values, and parenting in one’s children.[@fishkinJusticeEqualOpportunity, p.39-40] Traditionally, FA is defined rather narrowly here, with factors such as the wealth and connections of a family left out of FA. This is done to minimize the overlap between FA and ELC, which states that social contingencies ought to be reduced as much as possible. Factors such as a family’s wealth and power therefore falls under the purview of ELC, and is accordingly equalized when the principle is applied. 
However, closer examination of the connection between the two concepts, with the aid of empirical evidence, suggest that ELC and FA are difficult to separate in the aforementioned manner. Specifically, the type of family a child is born into has wide-ranging impacts on the future success of the child. For instance, studies have shown how  family arrangements (two parent nuclear family, one parent family, same-sex parent families, etc.), class, and racial backgrounds differentially affect student outcomes.[@fisherComparativeStudyUnderachieving;  @potterPsychosocialWellbeingChildren2017; @vangelderenQualityLifeAdolescents2012; @lareauUnequalChildhoodsClass2003] Then, one must either shrink the concept of FA so much so that its is deflated beyond practical utility, or bite the bullet: accept that incompatibility of the two concepts.^FA-ELC
Already, one of the core tenants that is required in order for M to be differentiated from simply an aristocracy seems to prominently conflict with a core liberal commitment.
## The Trilemma (Fishkin)
Having introduced the three core components of Fishkin’s theory, I will now introduce his trilemma, which states that the three core tenants of liberal thought (M, ELC, FA) cannot be maintained together simultaneously. Indeed, while individually, these principles are crucial for a liberal society, any combination of two of these principles results in the third being excluded. To sketch this out briefly,
- M + ELC entails that children must develop under equal conditions, which requires overriding FA. This implies strong state interference into the private sphere, with historical analogies occurring in states which can hardly be considered liberal, for example, early Soviet education policy (1920s–30s) and the Chinese Cultural Revolution.[@figesRevolutionaryRussia189119912014, p. 141-145; /]
- M + FA entails that children from more well-off families will systematically develop superior qualifications, violating ELC. This phenomenon is well-documented empirically.[@markovitsMeritocracyTrapHow2019]
- As previously stated, the case of ELC + FA is self-defeating.
So, no meritocracy society satisfies both ELC and FA at the same time.
## The Diachronic Turn

- **Reframe the starting point charitably.** Grant, against the empirical evidence just established, that some corrective mechanism achieves ELC at $t_0$. 
	- This is a deliberate concession, not a retraction - flag it as such explicitly.
- **Run M at $t_0$.** Under stipulated ELC, $M$ is justified: rewards track genuine merit. Winners receive wealth, status, and social capital.
- **Activate FA as the transmission mechanism.** Winners exercise FA - investing differentially in their children. This is not a contingent failure of policy; it is the ordinary, legally protected exercise of parental autonomy that any liberal society permits.
	- This must be protected.
- **State the key move: M funds the FA that destroys ELC.** At $t_1$, ELC is violated not by an external factor but by $M$'s own outputs. The winners $M$ produced are precisely the agents whose FA generates the next generation's unequal starting points. $M$ is the causal source of its own justificatory failure.
- **Apply the justificatory standard recursively.** $M$ is justified if and only if ELC holds. ELC does not hold at $t_1$. Therefore $M$ at $t_1$ is unjustified by the very principle that justified $M$ at $t_0$. The standard is self-applying and self-defeating.
- **Distinguish from the static dilemma explicitly.** The static argument shows FA∧ELCFA \wedge ELC FA∧ELC is empirically unachievable. The diachronic argument is independent and stronger: even under the most favorable conditions the static argument rules out — stipulated ELC at t0t_0 t0​ — MM M actively generates the inequality that defeats it through FA. The failure is internal to MM M's logic, not merely an external empirical constraint.
	- Note that the charitable stipulation of ELC at t0t_0 t0​ does not require equalizing natural contingencies, only social ones. The mechanism that violates ELC at t1t_1 t1​ operates through FA-transmitted social contingencies — parental investment, cultural capital, differential parenting — not through natural contingencies, which remain constant across generations. The diachronic argument therefore holds even if natural contingencies are left entirely unaddressed.
- **Close by ruling out the "reset" escape route.** A defender might say: re-impose ELC at $t_1$ through redistribution. But this concedes that $M$ requires permanent external correction to sustain its own justificatory conditions - meaning $M$ is not a self-standing principle of justice but a temporary corrective mechanism dependent on the very egalitarian intervention its defenders typically oppose.

## The Key Distinction from the Rawlsian Luck Objection

* Rawls's objection is *synchronic*: at a single moment, your talent is lucky, so you don't deserve the reward.  
* This argument is *diachronic*: even granting (against Rawls) that the initial allocation was fully deserved (or, not against Rawls, since everybody starts exactly the same, i.e., the strong ELC thesis), the system's own outputs poison its future inputs.  
* The two arguments are logically independent. You need not accept the luck objection to accept this one. This argument is stronger than the luck objection since its has less assumptions.

## The Conclusion

* Meritocracy is not simply "fair" or simply a "myth." It is a normative system that, under the liberal constraint of FA, necessarily generates the structural inequality that defeats its own justificatory conditions across generations.  
* The prompt's binary is therefore a false dichotomy: meritocracy begins as a fairness claim and ends as inherited privilege wearing a meritocratic disguise \-- not because of external corruption, but through its own internal logic.
* If we want to preserve meritocracy, the right question to ask is is: are we willing to give up the very foundations of the liberalism in order to do so?

## Craft Issues the Essay Must Solve

- **Define ELC with surgical precision.** The argument's force depends entirely on how "Equality of Life Chances" is specified. James Fishkin's original formulation is the **statistical/predictive** reading: a person's life prospects should not be predictable from morally arbitrary facts about their birth. This is a demanding conception. A weaker reading — equal formal access, or Rawlsian "fair equality of opportunity" (FEO) permitting family-transmitted advantages that don't violate a competitive-access principle — can absorb the diachronic pressure without collapse. **Rivera López (2017, "¿Es el 'trilema de Fishkin' un verdadero trilema?")** argues explicitly that the trilemma dissolves on moderate readings of the three principles. The essay must _own_ the strong reading of ELC and justify why the weaker readings are inadequate (e.g., that they already concede the fairness justification to contingency).

## Node-by-node Refutations

### Node 1: The Biconditional "M is Justified Iff ELC holds"

**This is the most attackable premise, and the strongest objections live here.**

- **Zaitchik's Moorean move ("On Deserving to Deserve," _Philosophy & Public Affairs_ 6.4, 1977, 370–388)** is the decisive challenge. [@zaitchikDeservingDeserve1977] Zaitchik's target is the regress principle "If A deserves X in virtue of Y, then A must also deserve Y." He rejects it as a suppressed premise: one can deserve the outcome of one's effort without deserving the capacity to exert effort. If Zaitchik is right, **desert does not require ELC as a precondition** — it requires only that the agent exercises agency in the relevant way. This dissolves the biconditional; M is justified by desert directly, not conditionally through ELC. The essay's biconditional then becomes a controversial substantive claim, not a definition. _How to answer_: concede that Zaitchik refutes the _strongest_ regress version, but argue that in social (not interpersonal) contexts, distributive desert claims still require fair background conditions because the _institutional_ legitimacy of the distribution — not merely the agent's personal claim — is at stake. This is the position of **Huub Brouwer, "Desert in liberal justice: beyond institutional guarantees" (_Canadian Journal of Philosophy_ 46.2, 2016, 248–267)**, though Brouwer ultimately uses it to _defend_ desert against Cohen.
	- My claim is not that no one ever deserves anything on a personal level, its just that any desert-based justification for meritocracy fails on a societal/institutional level because it is self-defeating. There is already literature that support the idea that there is a difference between institutional level desert and personal desert:
		- [@feinbergDoingDeservingEssays1970, p. 55-94] which distinguishes between institutional entitlements and genuine personal desert.
		- [@pojmanWhatWeDeserve1999, p. 186-95] which distinguishes between “p-desert,” i.e., a pre-institutional moral claim that describes what a person deserves in virtue of their character, effort, or conduct, and “i-desert,” i.e., what a person deserves relative to the rules of an existing institution.
- **Mulligan's essentiality of origin argument (_Justice and the Meritocratic State_, Routledge 2018, ch. 9 "Defending Desert from John Rawls")** [@mulliganJusticeMeritocraticState2017] is the second major attack. Using Kripke's essentiality thesis, Mulligan argues that one's innate traits could not have been otherwise, hence they are not "arbitrary" in the morally relevant sense, hence the luck objection collapses. If the luck objection collapses, the normative grounding of ELC as a necessary condition for M also weakens — ELC becomes desirable but not strictly constitutive of M's justification. **This is the hardest version of the attack on Node 1 to answer on the essay's own deontological terrain.** The response is well-rehearsed: **Napoletano, "Can Essentiality of Origin Save Meritocracy From The Luck Objection?" (_Philosophia_ 51.2, 2022, 883–895)** argues that essentiality of origin conflates metaphysical necessity with moral non-arbitrariness, and that the market contingencies determining _which_ talents pay off remain luck-dependent even if the talents themselves are essential. Citing Napoletano lets the essay dispatch Mulligan in a footnote rather than a paragraph.
	- See [@napoletanoCanEssentialityOrigin2022]^essentiality-of-origin-refuted

### Node 2: Family Autonomy as an Affirmed Liberal Principle

- **Brighouse and Swift's _Family Values_ (Princeton, 2014)** [@brighouseFamilyValuesEthics2014] is the sophisticated objection here. They argue that FA is _not_ a unitary principle but decomposes into (a) "familial relationship goods" realised through intimate partialities like bedtime stories, conversation, moral modeling — which are constitutive of valuable parent-child relationships — and (b) transferable positional advantages like elite schooling and bequest, which are not constitutive of family value. Only (a) generates genuine liberal duties of non-interference; (b) can be regulated, taxed, or abolished without damaging the family as such. If this decomposition works, **the trilemma collapses from the FA side**: liberal society can affirm "thin" FA (protecting relationship goods) while rejecting "thick" FA (positional transmission), and the diachronic feedback loop loses most of its force because the $t_{1}$ winners cannot legitimately translate their rewards into equality-destroying advantages for their children.
	- First to clarify, this distinction is present in the trilemma framework, where (b) is accounted for under ELC, and (a) essentially represents FA.
	- There is research (as I’ve noted in [[A Diachronic Rejection of Meritocracy#^parenting-effects]]) which suggests that even these immaterial things as described in (a) has real effect on the distribution of wealth. Here are some more sources to help refute this idea: [@segallIfYoureLuck2011; @gheausWhatAbolishingFamily2018]. In essence, Brighouse-Swift's decomposition fails empirically; the "constitutive" partialities produce the same life-chance divergence as the "positional" ones. The diachronic argument therefore survives even thin FA.^brighouse-swift-counterargument-refutation ^brighouse-swift-counterargument-refutation
		- `This could possibly be its own paragraph.` 

### Node 3: The Trilemma Structure Itself

- **Rivera López, "¿Es el 'trilema de Fishkin' un verdadero trilema?"** (available on academia.edu, in Spanish but summarisable in English) argues the trilemma is an artefact of _extreme_ interpretations of all three principles. On moderate readings — FA as limited parental partiality, M as presumptive rather than absolute merit-tracking, ELC as rough rather than strict statistical equality — the three are compossible. The essay must take a stand: either the moderate readings don't capture what's normatively interesting about each principle (the preferred response, because moderate M is not really _meritocracy_ in the grand-normative-theory sense the question asks about), or the moderate readings still face the diachronic problem albeit in attenuated form.
	- `This will be ignored, since it is not a major counteragument, and it is not that interesting.`
- **Joseph Fishkin's own resolution in _Bottlenecks_ (2014, chs. 3 and 5)** [@fishkinBottlenecksNewTheory2014]is effectively an "escape through pluralism": abandon the goal of literal equal opportunity, focus instead on loosening the **bottlenecks** through which people must pass. On this view, the trilemma is a reason to **reject the unitary framework that produced it**, not a reason to reject meritocracy per se. This is the most philosophically sophisticated escape and the essay must either dismiss it (on the grounds that opportunity pluralism already concedes the anti-meritocratic point by abandoning ELC) or show that the bottleneck framework itself reproduces the diachronic problem one level down.
	- There are a few issues with this response:
		1. It requires deep institutional redesign, which may not be liberal. How would one ensure that people have value pluralism or that as many valued goods as possible should be non-zero-sum?
		2. This doesn't really solve the problem that FA poses. Here, it is claimed that once there is a diversity of paths one could take in order to achieve some desired position, that family advantage would be minimized. But this is not necessarily true. If there are nn n options to reach a desired social position, the winners of each round are still those whose FA-transmitted advantages map best onto whichever of the nn n paths they pursue. Multiplying paths does not eliminate the transmission mechanism; it merely distributes it. For lim⁡n→∞n\lim_{ n \to \infty } n limn→∞​n, the criteria for a desired social position become so amorphous and undefined that the system is no longer a meritocracy in any meaningful sense.
### Node 4: The Diachronic Turn

- **The strongest objection here is an analogue to G.A. Cohen's critique of Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument** (_Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality_, Cambridge 1995, ch. 1; _Rescuing Justice and Equality_, Harvard 2008) [@cohenSelfownershipFreedomEquality1995]. Nozick famously argued that any patterned distributive principle is destabilised by free transactions — a structurally identical diachronic argument but pointing the other way (against egalitarianism, for entitlement theory). Cohen's response is that a just society requires not merely just institutions but a **justice-supporting ethos** that regulates individual choice. Applied reflexively to the student's argument: the meritocrat can respond that the diachronic erosion occurs only because parents exercise FA _in an ethos-less way_; a meritocratic ethos could restrain parental transmission of advantages without requiring coercive abolition of FA. This is essentially the Brighouse-Swift point dressed in Cohen's clothing.
	- See [[A Diachronic Rejection of Meritocracy#^brighouse-swift-counterargument-refutation]]. The same logic applies here. Cohen's ethos-constrained parent who only reads bedtime stories still produces the $t_{1}$ inequality, because the ethos-permissible intimacies are themselves the mechanism of advantage transmission.
- **The essentiality-of-origin objection resurfaces here too.** Mulligan could argue that even at t₁, the children of winners have essentially their innate traits, so the inequality of _opportunity_ at t₁ is only an inequality of _developmental inputs_, not an inequality of morally deserved outcomes. The Napoletano reply handles this: developmental inputs are precisely what ELC tracks, and the feedback loop operates through developmental inputs, not through magical transmission of merit itself.
	- See [@napoletanoCanEssentialityOrigin2022]

### Node 6: The Escape Routes

- **Drop FA**: The essay correctly notes Mulligan concedes this. In _Justice and the Meritocratic State_ Mulligan explicitly argues that inheritance should be eliminated in a true meritocracy (see ch. 8 on "Public Spending and the Establishment of Equal Opportunity"; the review in _3 Quarks Daily_ summarises this clearly). The essay's strongest move is to make this concession _vivid_: the most committed contemporary defender of meritocracy agrees that the family must be radically reformed, which is itself evidence that meritocracy cannot coexist with ordinary liberal commitments to the family.
	- In contemporary literature, this is most comprehensively spelled out in [@mulliganJusticeMeritocraticState2017, sec. 4.5; 8.4] which makes an argument for limiting the autonomy of the family, including through mandatory vaccination, regulation/prohibition of home education, heavy regulation of homeschooling, and “epistemic modesty,” with a catch-all requirement for parents to defer to expert consensus, or the state. Even though he tries to be modest with these changes, they are quite restrict by liberal standard, violating principles such as the right to religious freedom. He also advocates for far higher tax rates (even approaching 100% in some cases) and the elimination of inheritance, which undermines the very foundation of a liberal, market economy.
- **Weaken ELC**: This is the Joseph Fishkin / anti-bottleneck route. The reply is that weakening ELC from "equal life chances" to "non-bottlenecked plural opportunities" abandons the fairness-based justification of meritocracy. Meritocracy was meant to justify _inequalities of outcome_ as deserved; if we no longer claim that the background enabled desert, the outcomes are not deserved but merely non-arbitrarily distributed — a weaker and different claim. **Andersson, "Meritocracy: From Starting Gate to Bottlenecks" (_The Journal of Ethics_ 29.5, 2025, 819–845)** makes exactly this trade-off explicit: meritocrats must choose between desert-sensitivity (with intractable luck problems) and an anti-bottleneck principle (which abandons desert-sensitivity). Andersson defends the latter; the essay can cite Andersson as evidence that even a sympathetic recent philosopher admits meritocracy must abandon its desert-based fairness claim to survive. **This is a very useful citation: it's post-2020, engages Fishkin directly, and a meritocrat himself concedes the essay's key point.**
	- See [@anderssonMeritocracyStartingGate2025], who is generally sympathetic to the meritocratic view, and who highlights the trade off between desert-sensitivity and the anti-bottleneck principle. In short, one must choose to either: (1) abandon the desert claims of meritocracy substantially, or (2) face intractable problems presented by the trilemma and luck objection.
- **Treat M as temporary**: The essay's framing is correct — this concedes that M is not a background condition of justice. Add: a temporary M is effectively a transitional justice mechanism, which means the _justifying_ framework is whatever normative principle picks out the target state M is transitioning toward. Meritocracy becomes instrumentally justified, not foundationally justified, which is the essay's conclusion.
	- This is very heavily diluting the concept of M, and it becomes instrumentally justified. This is what I would accept.
