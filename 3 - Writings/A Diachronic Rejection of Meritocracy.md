## Introduction
In contemporary discussions of meritocracy, two perspectives are common. Either meritocracy is the champion of individual abilities and effort, or it is a sham, a societal misconception which only entrenches existing power and wealth differentials. I argue that this is a false binary: meritocracy can be both, *at difference times.* This argument is developed within the context of liberal societies—the only context in which meritocracy is seriously advanced as a fairness claim, since it presupposes commitments to individual liberty and family autonomy that liberalism independently affirms.[^1] Through a diachronic analysis, we will find that meritocracy is either illiberal, or fundamentally an incoherent, self-defeating concept. 
In this paper, I specifically attack desert-based meritocracy, the principle that the allocation of social goods is intrinsically justified because individuals deserve outcomes proportional to their merits. [@mulliganMeritocracy2023, sec. 2.2] I concede that instrumental merit, i.e., it is clear that allowing the most qualified to hold a position is a sound principle, regardless of how one is able to become the most qualified, is valid to a certain degree.[^2]
**In short, meritocracy, by necessity, it promotes an unacceptable level of structural inequality, which gives us good normative reasons to reject it as a viable principle of societal organization.**
## Justifying Meritocracy
Desert-based meritocracy (DBM) claims to be a *just* system of allocation where positions and rewards go to those who earn them through relevant talent and effort, disregarding irrelevant characteristics.[@fishkinJusticeEqualOpportunity, p. 22] But what does it mean to be just? We can distinguish between two interpretations.[@fishkinJusticeEqualOpportunity, p. 22; @rawlsTheoryJusticeRevised1999, sec. 47]
	(1) *Procedural justice* states that if the procedure involved in the distribution of social goods is justice, then the outcome of that distribution is just. John Rawls’ terms this “pure procedural justice,” giving the analogy of a fair gambling game, where as long as the procedure is carried out according to fair rules, the outcome is just—though it may result in a differential distribution of goods. [@rawlsTheoryJusticeRevised1999, p. 74-76] 
	(2) *Background justice* regards the circumstances which must hold for procedural justice to be meaningful, that is, that preexisting institutions must be fair before the procedure executes, otherwise the procedure just launders whatever inequalities preceded it. 
It seems that any strong meaning of “justice” must satisfy the criteria from both interpretations of the term. Disregard the former, and there is no guarantee that the the people who are the hardest working are compensated for their efforts. Disregard the latter, and the society merely entrenches extant inequities over time. 
Therefore, to be a just system of distribution, meritocracy must satisfy justice in this strong sense. 
Let’s consider (2) in more specificity. Following the convention first explicated in Rawls’s *A Theory of Justice*, we can distinguish between two why in which background justice may be violated: natural contingencies and social contingencies.[@rawlsTheoryJusticeRevised1999, sec. 12] Natural contingencies represent differences in ability which are innate, broadly encompassing traits such as strength, intelligence, and creativity. Social contingencies represent differences in ability which are due to social circumstances outside one’s control, such as the one’s family wealth, class background, and level/quality of schooling.
Background justice for DBM therefore requires equalization of social contingencies specifically; call this Equality of Life Chances (ELC), following Fishkin.[@fishkinJusticeEqualOpportunity, p. 32-34] Social contingencies have no bearing on one’s merits, and simply launder pre-existing inequality while talent and effort are themselves natural endowments, and to require their equalization would dissolve M entirely, since there would be nothing left to reward. In fact, this is not merely a stipulation: it reflects the meritocrat's own preferred account, shared by Mulligan and Miller alike, that natural talent is the legitimate desert base and social circumstances are the distorting factor.[@millerTwoCheersMeritocracy1996; @mulliganJusticeMeritocraticState2017, sec. 4.3] 
In short, we can define what I term the *necessary condition*; that is, M is normatively justified if and only if ELC holds. On this note, however, the meritocrat may argue that the argument proves too much. Alan Zaitchik argues that, since it seems like a pre-theoretical certainty that some people deserve things, any argument whose conclusion is “nobody deserves anything” must have a false premise.[@zaitchikDeservingDeserve1977] But this objection misidentifies the target. The biconditional is an institutional claim, not a claim about personal desert. It claims that any desert-based justification for meritocracy fails at the societal and institutional level, because the system is self-defeating. In fact, this distinction is well-established in the literature: Feinberg distinguishes between institutional entitlements and genuine personal desert,[@feinbergDoingDeservingEssays1970, p. 55-94] and McLeod separates “p-desert,” or a pre-institutional moral claim grounded in a person's character, effort, or conduct, from “i-desert,” or what a person deserves relative to the rules of an existing institution.[@pojmanWhatWeDeserve1999, p. 186-95] We can granting Zaitchik that individuals have genuine personal desert claims while leaving the institutional biconditional entirely untouched. So, the necessary condition stands.
## The Necessary Condition
Liberalism generally upholds the principle of Family Autonomy (FA): that parents the right to invest time, attention, culture, values, and parenting in one’s children.[@fishkinJusticeEqualOpportunity, p.39-40]
Some have objected to the entire concept of FA. Harry Brighouse and Adam Swift have argued that FA is not a unitary principle but decomposes into (a) “familial relationship goods,” realized through intimate partialities like bedtime stories, conversation, moral modeling, and (b) transferable positional advantages like elite schooling and bequest, which are not constitutive of family value. Only (a) generates genuine liberal duties of non-interference; (b) can be regulated, taxed, or abolished without damaging the family as such.[@brighouseFamilyValuesEthics2014] In fact, I accept this decomposition by defining FA rather narrowly, with factors such as the wealth and connections of a family left out. Factors such as a family’s wealth and power instead falls under the purview of ELC. Still, there is research which suggests that even these immaterial things as described in (a) has real effect on the distribution of wealth; in other words, that ELC and FA are difficult to separate. Specifically, the type of family a child is born into has wide-ranging impacts on the future success of the child. For instance, studies have shown how family types,[^3] class, racial backgrounds, and even parenting styles significantly affect student outcomes.[@fisherComparativeStudyUnderachieving;  @potterPsychosocialWellbeingChildren2017; @vangelderenQualityLifeAdolescents2012; @lareauUnequalChildhoodsClass2003; @segallIfYoureLuck2011; @gheausWhatAbolishingFamily2018] So, it seems that Brighouse-Swift's decomposition fails empirically. If this is the case, then, given the the necessary condition and FA, we can deduce that M is incompatible with liberal societies. 
The meritocrat might raise two objections here:
1. The meritocrat accepts the empirical evidence, but argues that it is evidence of a fixable policy, not a structural incompatibility.
2. The ELC does not require perfect equalization, only that life chances are not predictable from morally arbitrary facts beyond some acceptable threshold.[@lopezEsTrilemaFishkin1996] %% the trilemma is an artefact of extreme interpretations of all three principles. On moderate readings — FA as limited parental partiality, M as presumptive rather than absolute merit-tracking, ELC as rough rather than strict statistical equality — the three are compossible.  %%
## The Diachronic Argument
%%Possibly include a refutation based on the fact that many of the equalizations suggested here would be illiberal if there is space.%%
To address these concerns, I will develop an argument that demonstrates the conceptual incompatibility of FA and ELC, which I term the *diachronic argument*. Assuming a liberal society and granting that all persons are equalized at some initial time ($ELC_{t_{0}}$) and that at $t_{0}$ FA is irrelevant ($\lnot FA_{t_{0}}$; e.g., all families have the exact same structure and parent in the exact same manner), we can imagine the following to occur:
1. $M$ operates from $t_{0} \rightarrow t_{1}$ under perfectly fair conditions.
2. $M$ produces winners (due to people’s natural contingencies), who are then free to exercise $FA$ at $t_{1}$.
3. $FA$ means that parents are free to investment differentially in children, which means $\lnot ELC_{t_{2}}$.
4. Operating $M$ once again, and since the winners of $M$ continue to differentially invest in their progeny, furthering $\lnot ELC$, $\neg\,{ELC}_{t_2} \rightarrow \neg\,{ELC}_{t_3} \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow {ELC}_{t_n}$.
5. Therefore, even granting ${ELC}_{t_0}$, ${FA}_{t_1} \rightarrow \neg\,{ELC}_{t_n}$ for all $n \geq 2$.
6. Then, since it fails to satisfy the necessary condition, liberal societies must reject $M$.
Importantly, this argument is different from Rawls’ luck objection. The luck objection states that, *at a single moment,* your talent is based on arbitrary and thus morally irrelevant factors.[@logarRawlssRejectionPreinstitutional2013] Thus, you cannot be said to deserve the rewards that flow from those talents, and the basic structure should not allow them to determine distributive shares unchecked. The diachronic argument, however, grants that the initial allocation of rewards was fully deserved, and shows instead that M is necessarily self-refuting. The injustice is therefore not located in the arbitrariness of talent at a single moment, but in the very structure of the intersection of M and liberal societies. This means that one can reject the luck objection entirely and yet accept the diachronic critique; in fact, this argument is stronger than the luck objection since it offers more concessions to the meritocrat.
## Some Considerations
Returning to the aforementioned concerns, as long as one accepts the presence of natural contingencies, the diachronic argument ensures that M eventually undermines itself. The meritocracy might then argue for the equalization of natural contingencies in order to resolve the argument, but this faces several problems. First, it is unclear whether this would be liberal in nature; its hard to see how a society could equalize something like IQ or creativity without massive intrusions into the rights of families to their own children. Second, there are some natural contingencies which are extremely difficult, if not impossible, to mitigate. For instance, the location or time at which someone is born. It would be extremely challenging for a society to be able to coordinate the location and time of birth of every individual—yet, one’s birth location is clearly relevant for one’s future prospects, and thus must be mitigated given the overarching goal of mitigating natural contingencies.[@shacharBirthrightLotteryCitizenship2009; @rawlsTheoryJusticeRevised1999, sec. 12]
Having examined the specific objections towards the incompatibility between FA and ELC, let us examine some other, a general defenses of meritocracy. In *Justice and the Meritocratic State*, Thomas Mulligan presents a rather unique defense of meritocracy, arguing from a metaphysical front. Drawing from Kripke’s concept of the essentiality of origin,[@kripkeNamingNecessity1980, p. 111-116] Mulligan argues that since one’s innate traits are metaphysically necessary to your identity, those traits are therefore not “arbitrary” in the morally relevant sense. One could not wish to be born with different talents, since the person with different talents would not be you. Napoletano (2022), however, demonstrates that this argument conflates metaphysical necessity with moral non-arbitrariness: that a trait could not have been otherwise does not entail that it is a fair basis for distributing social rewards.[@napoletanoCanEssentialityOrigin2022] But this refutation is in fact unnecessary for the basic function of the diachronic argument, since it operates through social contingencies. Natural contingencies are only relevant at $t_{0}$, though it then begins to produce social contingencies which become the main problem for the meritocrat.
To address one final objection, a meritocrat might argue for the re-imposition of ELC after the application of FA in each time period. But this concedes that M requires permanent external correction to sustain its own justificatory conditions, meaning $M$ is not a self-standing principle of justice but a temporary corrective mechanism dependent on possibly illiberal interventions of state to restore a favorable condition. It is hard to see any meritocrat accepting this as a satisfactory interpretation of meritocracy.
## Conclusion
Framing meritocracy as a binary is a false dichotomy. It fundamentally creates the situation for its own demise, even when it’s given the charitable concessions at to begin with. So, if we want to preserve meritocracy, the right question to ask is is: are we willing to give up the very foundations of the liberalism in order to do so?
