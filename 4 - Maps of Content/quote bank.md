## [[Hamlet (play)|Hamlet (play)]]
- *Doubt thou the stars are fire,*
  *Doubt that the sun doth move,*
  *Doubt truth to be a liar,*
  *But never doubt I love.*
	- Hamlet from *Hamlet, (2.2.124-127)*
- *This above all: to thine own self be true,
  And it must follow, as the night the day,
  Thou canst not then be false to any man.*
	- Hamlet from *Hamlet, (1.3.82-84)*
- *For who would bear the whips and scorns of time,
  Th' oppressor's wrong the proud man's contumely,
  The pangs of despised love, the law's delay,
  The insolence of office, and the spurns
  That patient merit of th' unworthy takes, 
  When he himself might his quietus make 
  With a bare bodkin? (3.1.79-85)*
	- What is the purpose of living, if living is suffering? Answers to this questions can be found the various religions of the word (see Buddhism, for example), and in many philosophical schools of thought.
- *Which dreams, indeed, are ambition,
  For the very substance of the ambitious is merely
  The shadow of a dream. (2.2.276-278)*
	- This is an interesting phrase that seems could have multiple definitions. Ambitions here could be interpreted as a more concrete version of one's goals. This is because shadows seem to represent a more "grounded" (literal) version of a dream, as it touches the ground, while dreams are typically light and floating, rather ethereal in composition. 
*Purpose is but the slave to memory,
Of violent birth, but poor validity,
Which now, like fruit unripe, sticks on the tree,
But fall, unshaken, when they mellow be. (3.2.186-189)*

What we intend to to can be forgotten over the course of time, so that although we may be very passionate about something in the present, by some time in the future, the passion could have completely faded.

*But, orderly to end where I begun, 
Our wills and fates do so contrary run
That our devices still are overthrown. (3.2.208-210)*

The idea that nothing is Perfect, especially the execution of a plan. Though we may hope for something, what actually may occur is something else entirely.

*A certain convocation of politic worms are e’en at him. Your worm is your only emperor for diet. We fat all creatures else to fat us, and we fat ourselves for maggots. Your fat kind and your lean beggar is but variable service—two dishes, but to one table. That’s the end. (4.3.22-27)*

Hamlet describes again a sort of existentialism, questioning the purpose of existence. We do things for our own benefit, but ultimately, they are of no consequence. We will die one day, and all that we have done will be erased.

*O, that that earth which kept the world in awe/ Should patch a wall t’ expel the winter’s flaw! (5.1.222-223)*

This again references the fact that, no matter who you are, one day, you are doing to die and return to the earth, and be treated just the same as everyone else.

*What a piece of work is a man, How noble in reason, how infinite in faculty, In form and moving how express and admirable, In action how like an Angel, In apprehension how like a god, The beauty of the world, The paragon of animals. And yet to me, what is this quintessence of dust? (2.2.327-332)*  
*His virtues else, be they as pure as grace,
As infinite as man may undergo,
Shall in the general censure take corruption
From that particular fault. (1.4.. 35-38)*

This seems to echo the idea that a chain is only as strong as its weakest link. Here, Hamlet talks about the habit of his people to party, and he puts this as something that other nations critique them for. Thus, he concludes, despite their successes in other areas, this one fault ultimately corrupts their other virtues.

*For in the fatness of these pursy times
Virtue itself of vice must pardon beg,
Yea, curb and woo for leave to do him good. (3.4.171-173)*

Hamlet’s desperation and emotionality really shows through here, as he begs his mother to listen to him and repent for her actions.

*I must be cruel only to be kind. 
Thus bad begins and worse remains behind. (3.4.197-199)*

Masterful use of juxtaposition. Hamlet justifies his actions though a utilitarian framework, arguing that what he has done is only to prevent a further evil, though in the following sentence, he again contrasts that and indicates that regardless, worst is to come. While there is a certain sense of truth to this situation, I think that this also shows Hamlet’s true madness showing through.

*Why, let the stricken deer go weep,
The hart ungallèd play.
For some must watch while some must sleep.
So runs the world away.*

This is simply a fabulous quote, describing how there are some in the world that face the truth head on, while others (Claudius, in this case), escape, unable to face the truth.

*But such officers do the king best service in the end. He keeps them, like an ape, in the corner of his jaw, first mouthed to be last swallowed. When he needs what you have gleaned, it is but squeezing you and, sponge, you shall be dry again. (4.2.17-20)*

Hamlet compares Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to mere puppets of the king, something that makes sense given that every time they have appeared to Hamlet, they had been under the instruction of the king or were acting in his interest. Hamlet is justifiably angry about this.
