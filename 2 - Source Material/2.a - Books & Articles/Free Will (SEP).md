## Summary
A comprehensive article that discuss the concept of free will very broadly, serving as a literature review.
## Significance
This introduces the large arguments/positions within debates over free will.
## Annotations
### Background
- Plato’s vision of free will is inherently moral, as he views free will as the ability to do what is essentially challenging ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=2&selection=40,10,42,69&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.2]]).
	- Free will is choosing to pursue the good.
- Aristotle holds a similar view to Plato, argues that free will is the ability to do something that may be unpleasant or immediately undesirable ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=2&selection=48,2,50,15&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.2]]).
	- In this way, free will is an instrument for character building.
- Chrysippus argues that you do not have free will, but that the determined action that results is the result of your nature. In this way, the person still holds the moral responsibility for what they do, since though they do not necessarily control what they do, they choose whether to be “good” or “bad” people, which is reflected in their actions ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=3&selection=1,80,4,71&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.3]]).
- Aquinas considered free will to be the means by which we move towards a rational end. So we seem to have a pre-determined end, but the means by which we move towards that end is up for us to decide ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=3&selection=69,67,77,58&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.3]]).
### Problems
- There are two main problems associated with the problem of free will. 
	1. Morality is based on free will, and without free will, we cannot have morality ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=4&selection=72,0,72,99&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.4]]). 
	2. It seems like we have free will, and the lack of free will would undermine many aspects of the way in which we conceive of the world ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=4&selection=79,0,80,7&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.4]]).
		- What specifically free will conflicts with depends on the philosopher.
			- Theological
			- Metaphysical
			- Scientific
- There are three claims regarding free will that are widely, though not universally, agreed upon:
	1. The freedom to do otherwise and the power of self-determination ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=5&selection=8,85,9,36|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.5]]).
	2. Free will must mean that one is morally responsible for their actions. ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=5&selection=9,37,10,74|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.5]])
	3. That compatibilism is true ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=5&selection=13,0,15,7|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.5]]).
		- The two set argument of “classical compatibilists”:
			1. What is contrary to human freedom is not determinism, but rather, external impediments that prevent someone from doing what they want. 
				1. [[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=5&selection=22,4,24,40|For example, Hobbes contends that liberty is “the absence of all the impediments to action that are not contained in the nature and intrinsical quality of the agent” (Hobbes 1654 [1999], 38; cf. Hume 1748 [1975] VIII.1; Edwards 1754 [1957]; Ayer 1954).]]
				2. Under this view, the freedom to do otherwise simply consists of the ability to do otherwise given that you prefer the other option more strongly to your previous preference. It does not mean that you can force yourself to do something that you do not prefer.
			2. Any attempt to ground free will in a more concrete sense is impossible.
				1. This was attempted by some, who argues that freedom to do otherwise necessitates not just the ability to *act* otherwise, but also the ability to *will* otherwise.
				2. [[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=5&selection=78,55,80,74|With respect to classical compatibilists’ analysis of self-determination, they argued that self-determination requires that the agent—rather than his desires, preferences, or any other mental state—cause his free choices and actions.]]
				3. The problem with this view is that in order to change one’s will, one must be able to *will* to change a will, and this leads to infinite regression.
- Disputes about free will are often fundamentally about disputes over the nature of moral responsibility ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=6&selection=66,46,70,111&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.6]]).
## What is Free Will?
### Moral Responsibility
- Beside the two views of free will as either the ability to have acted otherwise, and the ability to have willed otherwise, there is a third school of thought: that the “up-to-meness” in free will is relevant only in so far as to justify moral responsibility ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=7&selection=12,93,15,91|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.7]]).
	- There are some who even claim that free will could be [[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=7&selection=16,38,17,21|‘the strongest control condition—whatever that turns out to be—necessary for moral responsibility’]]
- There are generally three schools of thought regarding the nature of moral responsibility:
	- Moral responsibility as *answerability*:
	- Moral responsibility as *attributability*:
	- Moral responsibility as *accountability*: argues that certain judgements about the nature of an agents (concerning their virtues and vices or ability for either), regardless of if they have free will, are fitting ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=7&selection=51,13,51,30|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.7]]).
- **Central Definition:** An agent is morally accountable for performing an action. The agent deserves praise if the action goes beyond what can be reasonably expected of the person and deserves blame if the action is morally wrong.
- Praising/blaming an agent consists of experiencing (or being at least disposed to experience) reactive attitudes such as gratitude, approbation, and pride in the case of something that is praiseworthy, and resentment, indignation, and guilt in the opposite case ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=7&selection=82,0,91,2|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.7]]).
	- These changes in attitude towards someone does not necessitate a change in the blamer/praiser’s outward attitude or actions.
- *Basic Desert*: That an agent ought to be blamed simply because they performed some action, and not because of any particular consequences of their action ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=8&selection=0,0,2,89|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.8]]).
	- The key here is that it seems that agents are only responsible for their actions in cases like this given that their actions were *up to them*, or, at the very least, there has to be some control over the individual’s actions.
### Freedom to do Otherwise
- **Simple Conditional Analysis:** An agent has the ability to do otherwise if and only if, were the agent to have chosen to do otherwise, they would have done otherwise ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=8&selection=72,0,81,19&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.8]]).
	- However, this definition has two problems: 
		- It is merely an analysis of free action, not free will. In other words, it only explains when an agent has the ability to *do* otherwise, not to *choose* otherwise.
			- This cannot be solved either if one were just to claim that the ability to choose otherwise is determined by a preference/desire to choose otherwise, since we don’t always choose the things that we want to choose ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=9&selection=34,0,35,92&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.9]]).
				- Do we always choose what we most strongly desire ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=9&selection=46,16,46,108&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.9]])? 
				- Replace the second condition with “would probably choose to do otherwise” or some variant of that, though then we would have to define the modalities, and also contend with the fact that we do not always choose what is most probable that we do.
		- There is a more fundamental problem, in that some agents who lack the freedom to do otherwise, yet satisfy the analysis first presented. 
			- For example, someone with an extreme fear of the outside would never choose to go outside. Yet, it is the case that if the agent were to choose to go outside, they would have.  
- **Categorical Analysis:** An agent has the ability to choose or do otherwise than $\phi$ if and only if, holding everything constant up to the point at which the agent makes a decision, it was possible that the agent chooses or does otherwise than $\phi$ at the moment of decision ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=9&selection=95,0,112,1&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.9]]).
	- This view of the ability to do otherwise solve the case of the man who fears going outside, yet runs into other issues.
	- This view is compatible with determinism, since being able to do something different requires that we are somehow able to change the laws of nature or change the circumstances of the world before we were born, both of which being seemingly impossible; this is the **Consequence Argument** ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=10&selection=39,0,43,79&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.10]]). 
		- Lewis argues that they are, in fact, compatible, given a distinction between: 
			- **Weak Thesis:** “An agent is able to do something such that, if they did it, a law of nature would be broken.”
				- Your action is simply a condition that, if met, implies a broken law, similar to saying: "If I jumped ten feet high, the law of gravity would be broken." This doesn't mean you _cause_ gravity to break; it means your ability implies a hypothetical scenario where the laws are violated (or different).
			- **Strong Thesis:** “An agent is able to do something such that, if they did it, would constitute the agent causing the laws of nature to break.”
		- Compatibilists need only to appeal to the weak thesis, and not the strong thesis, as maintained by the incompatiblists ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=10&selection=81,106,101,1&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.10]]).
		- Some, including Van Inwagen, argue that Lewis’ claim is still too incredible. The article states that it may be too weak.
			- Lewis’s account seems to make some unintuitively suspect arguments, since he essentially claims that in the following propositions, all premises are compatible with one another:
				- John is able to raise his hand. 
				- A necessary condition for John’s raising his hand fails to obtain (i.e., that the laws of nature or past are different than they actually are).
				- John is not able to do anything that would constitute this necessary condition’s obtaining or cause this necessary condition to obtain (i.e., he is unable to do anything that would constitute or cause a law of nature to be broken or the past to be different).
			- It seems that it cannot be the case that both the first and the second statements are true, or the first and the third state are true, since in the case of the person with agoraphobia, “a necessary condition for John’s raising his hand fails to obtain” is true, since agoraphobia ensures that the agents is not able to choose to go outside. Yet, the first premise seems to suggest that the agent is able to go outside ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=11&selection=4,63,13,47&color=yellow|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.11]]).
### Sourcehood
- A different account of free will states that free will when an agent is the source of their own actions.
- The Frankfurt cases show this, as they represent situations whereby an individuals does not have the ability to choose otherwise, and yet, it seems that they are morally responsible for what they do ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=12&selection=28,0,36,59|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.12]]).
	- If we accept free will as the condition that is required for moral responsibility, that means that the Frankfurt cases additionally show that individuals have free will even when they lack the ability to do otherwise. 
- The Frankfurt cases allow us to draw two conclusions ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=12&selection=39,88,40,41|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.12]]):
	1. That the ability to do otherwise is not necessary for moral responsibility (and, if we accept the definition of free will as a means of ensuring moral responsibility, is not necessary for free will).
	2. That freedom and responsibility are the consequence of the *actual sequence*, in other words, what matters for moral responsibility is not what might have happened. Rather, it is what actually occured.
- There are some criticism of the Frankfurt cases however ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=12&selection=51,0,52,68|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.12]]):
	- For example, if it is the case that the decision made by an agent is deterministic, then incompabilists (most likely believers in libertarian free will, in this case) would reject the idea that the agent had any moral responsibility for their actions. 
	- If it is the case that the decision made by the agent is nondeterministic, then it must have been possible for the agent to have made a different decision, meaning that the agent retains the ability to do otherwise.
	- In either case, the agent cannot both be morally responsible in their actions and also be unable to do otherwise.
- If the Frankfurt cases are indeed successful, then they would show that free will and moral responsibility require at least some sense of an ability to do otherwise.
	- While the Consequence Argument presents a case against compatibilism, compatibilists can simply appeal to a form of ability to do otherwise that is relatively weaker (as opposed to the "all-in" version of the ability to do otherwise) ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=13&selection=23,78,27,79|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.13]];  [[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=13&selection=51,72,52,87|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.13]]).
- It doesn’t show that compatibilism is true, though it provides clear help to the compatibilist case.
	- In order to prove compatibilism, we have to actually prove that the kind of sourcehood that leads to free will is actually compatible with determinism ([[Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).pdf#page=13&selection=33,24,34,83|Free Will (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), p.13]]).
