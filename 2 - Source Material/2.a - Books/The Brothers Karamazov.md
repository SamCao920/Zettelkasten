## Summary
Dostoevsky’s masterpiece that explores faith through the tale of the Karamazov brothers.
## Significance
This is considered one of the foremost works of literature in world history.
## Characters
**Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov**
- Immoral, irreverent, and careless. He is a materialist who is ignorant of all things spiritual or metaphysical.
	- He is also a terrible father, since he neglects his children. 
		- He forgets that Sophia is Ivan’s as well as Alyosha’s mother. It’s almost like a “Fruedian slip” in that he likes Alyosha more than Ivan.
- Atheistic because he simply doesn’t care. It seems like he has no internal moral sense, and thus, he is not drawn to religion. So, it seems like although he attempts to justify his Atheism with rationality, the reasoning is sound but trivial, and the underlying motivation is simply a lack of a desire to be moral.
	- He criticizes the church for there perceived 
- On the surface, he seems like a buffoon. In fact, this is how he often refers to himself.
	- However, he is surprisingly rational, though in a petty way. He doesn’t use his rationality for virtuous means. Rather, he uses it to acquire wealth and spend it with little consideration.
	- He has this tendency to enjoy diminishing himself, talking down about himself.
- Represents the “unconscious”; letting lose repressed emotions in Freudian terms.
	- Is not aware of the existence of the self (or is afraid of the contradictions apparent in his self), and tries to run away from attempting to define his own life through debauchery. 
**Alexandrovich Miusov**
- A “man of principle.”
	- Having traveled to the West, he is influenced by Western European ideas of liberalism. In this way, he is also an atheist, except his atheism comes from almost a  feeling of superiority over the people who do believe in faith.
	- He does have “principle,” so he does not reject religion on the same basis that Fyodor does. Rather, he rejects it because he finds it superstitious and irrational. Fundamentally, this is grounded upon his liberal values.
	- As a result of his principles, he seems to be far more (at least on the surface), open to different ideas. This is seen when he is in the church, where he often expresses his doubts about the institution of religion with internal thoughts, but never expresses them to anyone else.
**Ivan Fyodorovich Karamazov**
- Intellectual and brilliant. 
- In many ways represents a “Greek” way of life.
	- In many ways, he is a Platonist, always looking at the “idea” of something rather than the physical manifestation of that thing.
	- Attempts to achieve *reinheit*, purity in the meaning of “washing oneself clean.” 
	- Connects with this dislike of being dependent on others. In Ancient Grecian morality, it was viewed as servile and morally degrading to be dependent on another.
	- A world of forgiveness is morally repugnant to Ivan. He wants a world of vengeance, and rather the world have “unavenged suffering” than forgiveness. This is a result of his argument against the belief in God outline below.
	- Ivan’s rationality is what is preventing him from being able to be a good person. He always thinks to hard about something, which makes it difficult for him to actually do good things.
- Often acts as an observer, a bystander, seeking the catalogue and observe the occurrences of the world rather than participate in them.
	- This is also reflected not just in his physical actions, but in the way that he approaches his thinking: for instance, he seems perfectly able to hold contradictory views on the nature of religion and belief, making arguments for each side, yet not believing in any.
	- He is able to build moral architecture (his argument for the takeover of the state by the church, which would end crime), despite not having any moral reservations himself. This torments him throughout the book.
	- Ivan’s jokes while he talks about the meaning of life and religion is his way of distancing himself (perhaps unconsciously) from these topics.
	- He finds it impossible to “love thy neighbor” because he himself is unable to get close to anyone else. He reasons that this is because the “cruelty and evilness of man” prevents him from loving his neighbors.
- Independent, and does not want to rely on anybody.
	- He believes that he is above everybody else, so he argues for the takeover of the state by the church, since that would mean that the “weak masses” would have moral guidance. This view is what he espouses through the story of the Grand Inquisitor.
> [!QUOTE] Ivan, “The Brothers Make Friends”
> Very, I want to get to know you once for all, and I want you to know me. And then to say good-bye. **I believe it's always best to get to know people just before leaving them.**
- Attempts to repress the “base” side of his nature and embrace the “noble” side. It, however, it seems that his base side still shows through at times. 
	- When he is describing the torture of children, he says how there is a part of all of us that desire the same sadistic feeling of being able to torture another. What this says is that this desire is also a part of him, and something notable enough that he uses it to make a general claim about the nature of humanity (which may or may not be true).
	- His argument for only living until thirty is again another performance of morality, the suppression of his base desire to lengthen his life like Fyodor.
- He makes an “anti-mystic” argument against belief in God.
	- Ivan’s argument from “Rebellion”: The “eternal harmony” which is God’s kingdom/heaven is built upon the sum of human history, which includes human suffering. This includes the suffering of children, who have not “eaten the apple” and are thus not responsible for the suffer that is brought upon them. A moral agent cannot accept the suffering of an innocent even if it brings them happiness. To participate in God’s world is to accept this arrangement. Therefore, while God may exist, Ivan chooses to “return the ticket.”
		- Reponses:
			- You are not a spectator that can simply “give back the ticket.” You are already a part of the world, and you cannot simply leave, because you are not a pure, innocent bystander.
			- Returning the ticket is not the right response.
			- Ivan is a sadist. He is not the innocent bystander. He enjoys watching kids suffer. So he can’t give back the ticket.
			- You should try to make the world better, not leave the world.
	- It’s not that “God’s motives are beyond human comprehension,” it is this very fact that he rejects. He believes in his human comprehension, and using it, he essentially finds God at fault for the suffering in the world.
	- He uses the argument from evil not to disprove the existence of God, but to prove why man ought not to believe in God, even if he does exist.
**Alexei Fyodorovich Karamazov**
- Intelligent, but not intellectual.
- Seeks purity and transcendence in life, although he attempts this in a vastly different way than Ivan. He is so invested in the spiritual that he seems to disregard the earthly (at least until father Zossima tells him to leave the monastery after his death).
	- He does, however, have a sensual side (he remains a “Karamazov”), which is revealed when he meets Katerina Ivanovna, is and stunned by her beauty.
		- This is again the case after Zossima’s death. As the miracle fails to appear with the body of Zossima stinking, Alyosha loses his faith for some time. 
		- This is only ended with his realization that love is powerful, as when he showed Grushenka love, she responded by changing herself, and regaining her perspective on life. This leads Alyosha on its own journey, where he reclaims his own faith.
- Represents a Christian way of life.
	- Attempts to achieve *lauterkeit*, purity in the sense of transformation of transfiguration.
	- His philosophy of Christian (active) love runs into issue when he loves both side of a conflict, and thus there is no clear way to support one or the other.
	- His unconditional love for other is sometimes misinterpreted as being ulterior motives.
		- His early interactions with Lise.
		- HIs early interactions with Ilyusha.
	- Interestingly, he seems to be partially convinced by Ivan during his conversation with him, and abandons some of the principle that are a part of his “Christian love” perspective.
> [!QUOTE] Alyosha in “The Confession of a 'Passionate Heart — in Verse”
> Alyosha was certain that no one in the whole world ever would want to hurt him, and, what is more, he knew that no one could hurt him. This was for him an axiom, assumed once for all without question, and he went his way without hesitation, relying on it.
- Dependent on others, but not such that others dislike him for his dependence. This is in line with his Christian morality, a morality that is much more about community and supporting one another.
- He withdraws from the world in order to get away from his sensual side, which seems to afflict every other Karamazov. 
	- At the same time, he seems aware of his sensual side, of the potential that he has as a “Karamazov.”
	- He is able to provisionally subsume all of his impulses and the need to face up to the contradictions in the self through his love of Zossima. He often hides behind Zossima, in essence hiding behind faith.
	- His rejection of his base side is what ultimately leads him to a sense of isolation in the world and his implicit guilt in the murder of his father.
**Dimitri Fyodorovich Karamazov**
> [!QUOTE] Dimitri in “The Confession of a 'Passionate Heart — in Verse”
> The awful thing is that beauty is mysterious as well as terrible. God and the devil are fighting there and the battlefield is the heart of man. But a man always talks of his own ache. 
- A person of contradictions, attempting to occupy the middle ground between Alexei and Ivan.
	- He is the only character that attempts to occupy the middle, shifting between angel and insect.
	- He is the only one who is genuinely trying to reconcile these two sides of himself.
	- His contradictions appear in everything that he does, the things that he experiences. He always is able to perceive both the “base” and the “noble” side of anything. 
		- “The ice burned my forehead like fire…”
		- “Kill oneself from delight…”
		- How, despite his hatred of Katerina Ivanovna, he was a “hair-breadth” from “the maddest love.”
- He is satisfied, even pleased when he heard of Katerina Ivanovna’s humiliation by Grushenka.
	- Shows how he is attracted to the raw sensuality of Grushenka, and not the far more upstanding Katerina.
- After he murders Fyodor, he seems to have a moment of realization that matches the moment of realization that occurred to Alyosha.
	- He is no longer as petty as he was before, although he remains emotional. He seems to have gained a larger perspective on life.
	- He wins Grushenka back from the Polish officer.
- Dimitri suffers for everyone in the world, since he acts is the paragon of what to do when faced with suffering. He accepts it, and digests it, and is able t take control of it. In doing so, he emerges joyful.
**Pavel Fyodorovich Smerdyakov**
- Epileptic & intelligent.
- Lacks the contradictions that are present within every other Karamazov (therefore, he is not even referred to as a Karamazov).
	- This could have been snuffed out with the cat-killing incident?
	- He has no sense of self that the other members of the Karamazov family do. He doesn’t have a “Karamazov” in him, and thus he is never referred to in that way.
- He is like Ivan without a superficial sense of morality.
	- He is a genuine sadist; everything he says is tinged with contempt.
	- In fact, Ivan is afraid of him. He scares Ivan during a conversation with him because he truly embodies what Ivan believes in. 
		- This exposes Ivan as a sort of Platonist intellectual, since he is afraid, on an instinctual/personal level, of the explication of his ideas.
- He has no personality, no eternal validity.
	- He mirrors Ivan as a result, intellectually and physically.
	- When Ivan is not there to support him intellectually, he falls ill.
**Lise Khokhlakov**
- Masochistic
- She is seems to be pure in the Christian sense.
	- She is an invalid (she suffers), and she is genuine with her emotions (e.g., her love letter to Alyosha).
	- This is part of the reason why Alyosha is in love with her.
**Katerina Ivanovna Verkhovtseva**
- The counterpart of Ivan. 
- She invents a reason to say in the “Dimitri drama.”
	- Although she loves Ivan, she plays up a sense of duty towards Dimitri. She enjoys the feeling of moral superiority over others through her self-laceration (it is a “self”-laceration since she choose herself to play this role of the suffering women who *must* protect her honor before her feelings).
	- The primary target of this “self”-laceration is Ivan, since Katerina is essentially inventing moral obligations in order to create moral barriers for him. In this way, she “lacerates” Ivan’s love for her and enjoys it.
**Elder Zossima**
- ~~The most enlightened character in the book.~~
	- He receives this enlightenment stance from his brother, Markel.
		- There is an idea of apostolic succession that is existentialized here, as Markel passes down his “sainthood” to Zossima, who passes it down to Alyosha. 
			- Each stage of this changes the form, bringing it closer to the truth.
	- The incident where he beats up its subordinate reminds him of the suffering of Markel and the things that he said to him. This changes him and makes him into the person that he would eventually become.
	- He joins a monastery, since that is the place where his radical spirituality would be protected.
		- Markel dies because his spirituality was not “protected,” and people just thought he was a crazy person.
		- But the retreat to the monastery still faces the problem of “Platonism.”
			- Zossima is not actually in the world, doing something. He is merely relegated to a monastery. And he realizes this, which is why he calls for Alyosha to leave the monastery.
- ~~The only character who realizes that the two sides of the soul are equal to one another, that no one should trump another, and they should exist in harmony.~~
	- Becoming a monk is the only means of shedding oneself of superfluous and unnecessary desires.
		- But this does not mean having no desires. It means having the right amount of desire, which is rather Aristotelian.
	- If this is the case, then why is he a monk? Why does he only seem to pursue the “noble” side of the soul?
- He wants to suffer for all of the sins of humanity. He thinks that the monks are the worst sort of people, and that is the reason that they have ended up in the monastery. They are the people who need the most saving.
	- We must always be doing good, and to everyone, since not doing so means “planting a seed of evil” in someone else.
	- A moral “butterfly effect” whereby an action has extended consequences, and an evil action plants a seed of evil in someone else, and the infection spread throughout society.
		- Therefore, one is responsible for everything. And, therefore, we must communally confess.
- Represents the ideals of the solution presented in the antinomies of the Grand Inquisitor.
	- And this is revealed after his experience of the duel and in his dealings with the mysterious visitor.
	- Represents the transformation of the individual through a sort of moral transformation, which is the solution to the contradictions from the Grand Inquisitor chapter.
		- The problem with the Grand Inquisitor is that the people are not transformed like Zossima.
- Hell is being unable to love. This refers to the actual Hell, though existentialized and demagicalized.
	- Sin means isolation, and thus confession to no one or just one person (Catholic view) doesn’t help. It requires group confession/confessing to the world/
	- This is why he has such a different view of confession than the Catholic Church or the Protestants.
	- Confession allows one to release guilt and be able to love others again.
	- One is responsible for a lot more suffering than one may realize, even though it may not mean suffering for everyone on Earth.
	- He argues for the opposite of Ivan, where the state becomes the church through the process of active love. This is his doctrine of “Christian Socialism.”
		- Moral individualism is the opposite of Christian Socialism; it brings about “destructive" impotence.” This is exemplified by the onion story.
**Mikhail Osipovich Rakitin**
- Is the devil incarnate?
	- He tries to tempt Alyosha into after the death of Zossima, in a moment of weakness for him.
- Argues against Ivan that immortality is not needed for virtue. Rather, freedom, equality, and fraternity are needed.
**Agrafena Alexandrovna Svetlov**
- The personification of sensuality and desire, becoming a point of contention between Dimitri and Fyodor.
- Seems rather like the female double of Fyodor in her willingness to ignore social conventions and flaunt her senuality.
- She is able to be “saved” by Alyosha after his discussion with her and Rakitin.
	- Alyosha makes her understand her own self-worth. He suffers for her sins. He is able to address her respectfully even though she is a wicked women.
	- This is the “existentialized miracle”; transformation of a human soul through love.
**Captain Nikolai Ilyich Snegiryov and Ilyusha**
- A “Greek” family.
- Creates a family that serves as a contrast to the Karamazovs. They are moral, poor, and value honor above all else.
	- Nikolai is ashamed of having folded so quickly in the face of the money that Alyosha offered him, so he rejects it. He is also ashamed of having befriended Alyosha so quickly.
**Kolya**
- He is at first someone who performs a sort of intellectualism, having been influenced by Ratkitin. 
	- He is plagued by blatant contradictions. For instance, though he says that he loves Illusha, he never visits him under the very end.
- He is reshaped by Alyosha into someone who is more genuine and loving.
**The Mysterious Visitor**
- He wanted to kill Zossima, the only person who knew about his murder. Doing so would allow him to go back to his life as normal.
	- But it would also seal him in hell, as he has not committed two murders, killing his only path out of hell.
	- The split second choice not to kill Zossima marked a changed in the person, an indication that they can finally be saved.
## The Grand Inquisitor
- An antimony between the Roman Catholic Church and Protestantism.
	- But eventually there is going to to be a third way offered.
	- Represents the conflict between the base (represented by the GI) and the noble (represented by Jesus).
- The Grand Inquisitor argues that what Jesus expects of people is impossible.
	- Thinks that people in general are weak, rebellious, and slavish.
	- He gives people the freedom of choice, but does not understand that normal people are simply overwhelmed by this power.
	- It may work for the select few, like Jesus, but it won’t work for everybody else.
	- And this is where the Catholic Church steps in, as they serve as the earthly agent for the people, taking their freedom of choice away from them (taking on their burden) and making things easier for them.
	- The Catholic Church gives people happiness through their authority.
- Jesus believes that people, at least deep down, are good and will make the right choice. This is why he offers the choice to people.
	- Thinks that all people are lofty and good.
- While Jesus offers total freedom and individuality, the Catholic Church offers total authority and control. Both views seem extreme.
- The three antinomies:
	- Materialism & faith:
		- Jesus argues that you don’t need any food to be moral.
			- The person who exemplifies this is Father Ferapont. 
			- Ferapont, however, is a completely crazy person. He is presented in a way that makes him arrogant, wild, and prone to hallucinations.
			- So this is an argument against Jesus’s position.
		- The GI argues that you need food in order to be moral.
			- This is the position that is supported in the next section, since in Zossima’s story, he talks about how little the priests survive off of.
			- In fact (through the cherry jam examples), it is ok to desire things beyond just the necessities. It becomes only bad when these things become a crutch or an addiction.
			- This is similar to the golden mean of Aristotle? You should enjoy things, but do so in a moderate degree.
	- Community: does faith require others/community?
		- The Protestant view argues that this is not needed.
			- Luther: “Every man is his own pope.”
			- A Kierkegaardian view.
		- The Catholic view is that a central church is needed.
			- There needs to be one universal church so that people will stop killing each other, since they won’t have any more personal religious differences
	- Morality/confession:
		- The Lutheran view is that people do not need moral guidance and are free.
			- Suspending the ethical, the individual; can’t be confessed to others since they are uniquely individual.
		- The Catholic view is that people do, and thus they have created institutions for such guidance.
			- Every person gets moral guidelines by the church that has universal moral guidelines.
		- The former view means crushing isolation, and the latter means the loss of moral agency.
			- The example given of this is the mysterious stranger, who is totally isolated in his guilt. This guilt is only removed once he confessed to the whole village.
			- Hell is not fire and brimstone: it is an existential state of absolute isolation.
			- Confession is what allows us to exit this existential isolation, since it brings together the heavenly (the desire to be good) and the earthly (the reality of the crime). This represents the synthesis of the two aspects that have been introduced in the previous sections of the book as a contrast, i.e., the base and the noble, the insect and the Madonna.
				- Confession is not suffering consequences for one’s actions. It is rather a process of alignment, bringing together one’s feels and the way the world is around them.
	- Miracles:
		- The Lutheran view is that people don’t need miracles.
			- But it is ok to perform miracles if it doesn’t coerce belief.
		- The Catholic view is that miracles are needed to ensure that people continue to believe in the religion.
	- Mystery?
	- Authority?

## Meta
- To give an argument that Dostoevsky want to be convincing, he:
	- Makes the people in the story acknowledge the truth of an argument.
	- He puts an argument in the enemy of that view.
- Dostoevsky embarks on a journey to demagicalize Christianity since a magical faith coerces belief.
	- At the same time, he is not against miracles. He just thinks that they should be used out of love, not as a demonstration of power.
- Dostoevsky utilizes physical sickness as a demonstration of mental sickness:
	- Grushenka gets sick because of her suffering under her spiritual transition with the help of Alyosha.
