## Summary
Presents a philosophical defense of meritocracy. 
## Significance
This is important work that informs the philosophical nature of meritocracy as a defending point of view.
## Explanation
### Central Thesis: Justice is Realized when People Receive what They Deserve.
Mulligan is a pluralist about morality, but a monist when it comes to justice.
- Justice and morality are not the same thing.
- Additionally, being a pluralist about justice means that you can value different things (such as justice or efficiency) fairly, and not have an overwhelming bias towards one thing specifically.
### Three Requirements of Justice
1. A theory of justice should suggest an economy that is reasonably efficient.
	1. Essentially, since there is always going to be an equity/efficiency trade-off, we should not sacrifice all efficiency in the pursuit of equity (as Rawls might suggest).
2. A theory of justice should suggest morally attractive public policies.
	1. This is arrived through a process of reflective equilibrium.
`I think that this point is relatively weak. While Mulligan claims that a refutation like 'our moral intuition may be fickle when it comes to tax policy' I don't believe that there is an 'intuition' in the same sense when it comes to tax policy at all. There may be an intuition that 'equal distribution is good', but an intuition of the kind 'the rich should be taxed x%' seems far stretched.`
3. A theory of justice must pass the Singer Test.
	1. The **Singer Test** states that moral opportunity costs of any public policy must be factored in. He quantifies this in terms of the number of lives potentially saved.
### Ideal and Non-ideal Justice
There are essentially four challenges that non-ideal theories pose towards ideal theories, which Mulligan states the ideal theories (such as meritocracy) is able to meet.’
1. **Feasibility:** A theory of justice must be reachable from the actual world given available resources.
2. **Policy guidance:** A theory of justice must be generate concrete and sensible policies that actually can serve as a policy guide. I.e., the policies that a theory creates must not unreasonably clash with our moral intuitions. ^policy-guidance
3. **Utopianism:** A theory must not be utopian in nature, meaning that it must not demand too much from human beings.
4. **Justificatory incompleteness:** A theory must be able to specify, down to the last details, its policy recommendations, and not remain in the realm of the abstract.
## Empirical Evidence
There is plenty of evidence to suggest that desert is a concept that is well-embedded in the human psyche, ever since we are born. It is even observed in many animals, suggesting a genetic origin.
Likewise, we can present a rejection of Rawlsian justice here, since empirically, people rarely choose to subscribe to his theories.
He then gives some reasons why we ought not to disregard intuition for desert specifically:
1. If it is the case the philosophy simply ought to systematize concepts that are already implicit in our intuitions (as a metaphilosophical principle) then it is the case that since there are already a plethora of empirical evidences that demonstrate the existence of intuition for meritocracy, our work is done.
2. Given reflective equilibrium, the role of intuition remains important.
3. A theory that does not have the backing of intuition cannot serve as a justified guidance to justice. See [[Justice and the Meritocratic State#^policy-guidance]] .
4. It is *dangerous* to violate intuitions about desert, since when people live under a system that they consider to be unjust, there is more likely to be social turmoil, destabilizing social cooperation.
5. (This last point seems to be an elaboration on the earlier intuition point)
### A Defense of Desert
First, he sweeps away two of Rawls’ other concerns regarding desert.
1. *Desert is impracticable, since we can never truly find out who truly deserves something.*
	1. But this is not true, since there are some deserts that are clearly observed (e.g., the fastest runner deserves the gold medals, and this is quantifiable).
`Even in cases where it is not immediately observable who has merit, we can look to the market for such an determination (e.g., the best artist is the artist whose art we are willing to pay the most for).`
2. *Desert is incompatible with public reason, that is, any distributive schemes must be justifiable to all reasonable citizens.*
	1. The best theory that is compatible with public reason is meritocracy.
Then, he outlines Rawls’ argument in the following manner:
- P deserves x on the basis of y only if P deserves y.
- If y originated in fortunate circumstances, then P does not deserve y.
- All putative desert bases originated in fortunate circumstances.
- Therefore, P does not deserve anything.
There are currently two standard responses to the above arguments:
1. **Zaitchik’s Moorean:** The above principle proves too much, since it would mean that no one ever deserves anything at anytime, which is clearly absurd (reductio).
	1. If I baked a cake, I do not even deserve to have my cake because it was due to fortuitous circumstances that I am even able to bake it in the first place.
2. **Sher:** Rejects premise 1, claiming that the regression of desert stops at a certain point, such as effort.
Mulligan proposes the following argument to strengthen their case: **he rejects premise 4, that all putative deserts originate in fortune. Drawing on the concept of the essentiality of origin, some properties of ourselves are simply metaphysically necessary so that we are ourselves. Therefore, it is not the case that *all* desert bases originate in fortune.**
- The error with Rawls is that he believes that people have robust identities totally independent of their traits, meaning that you could totally alter a people’s traits, but still come out with the same person. 
- This line of thinking also illustrates an intuitive difference between establishing equal opportunity through either:
	- Social policies, which mean perfectly reasonable,
	- And genetic modification of embryos to equalize natural abilities, which seems outrageous.
- An objection to the argument might be that since the assignment of genetics is purely arbitrary, it makes no sense to base desert off-of genetic assignment either.
	- But this argument runs into the same issue that the previous one did, namely, that it fails to consider genetics as something metaphysically necessary for the person.
