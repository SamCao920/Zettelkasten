## Summary
Modern classic that explores themes such as the nature of fate, human progress, revenge, existentialism, and religion.
## Significance
This is one of the most acclaimed works of fiction in all of fiction, and a work that (in my opinion) is deserving of it’s reputation.
## Themes and Ideas
- [[Our fate is determined]]
- [[As history progresses, morality advances as well]] 
	- This book serves as a repudiation of this idea, specifically regarding the technological advancements through history. This is also related to the contemporaneous ideas of the Enlightenment that Shelley would no doubt have been familiar with.
- [[It is not always good to know the truth]]
- [[The 'tragedy’ in tragic works of art often spring from the main character’s own actions]]
- [[Better to reign in Hell, then serve in Heaven]]
	- This is the attitude that the creature adopts throughout the entire book.
## Quotes
> [!PDF|yellow] [[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=74&selection=14,12,16,57&color=yellow|Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus, p.74]]
> > One man’s life or death were but a small price to pay for the acquirement of the knowledge which I sought for the dominion I should acquire and transmit over the elemental foes of our race.
- This seems be the main motivation for many characters in this work, and it is also what creates Frankenstein’s monster. 
- After coming back and visiting this quote after learning about Frankenstein’s discovery of the ability to create life, I think that the central motif of the story is one of humans attempting to conquer the unknown, although it often comes with dire consequences. This is exactly what the person writing the letters was attempting to do. 
> [!PDF|yellow] [[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=93&selection=5,11,11,39&color=yellow|Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus, p.93]]
> >  Besides, I had a contempt for the uses of modern natural philosophy. It was very different, when the masters of the science sought immortality and power; such views, although futile, were grand; but now the scene was changed. The ambition of the enquirer seemed to limit itself to the annihilation of those visions on which my interest in science was chiefly founded. I was required to exchange chimeras of boundless grandeur for realities of little worth.
> 
- This hints at his later attempts at “playing God” in his construction of Frankenstein. He was attracted to the grand undertakings of the alchemists and Hermetics, and not the incremental progress of the modern scientists.
> [!PDF|yellow] [[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=94&selection=0,1,10,27&color=yellow|Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus, p.94]]
> > The ancient teachers of this science,’ said he, ‘promised impossibilities, and performed nothing. The modern masters promise very little; they know that metals cannot be transmuted, and that the elixir of life is a chimera. But these philosophers, whose hands seem only made to dabble in dirt, and their eyes to pore over the microscope or crucible, have indeed performed miracles. They penetrate into the recesses of nature, and show how she works in her hiding-places. They ascend into the heavens: they have discovered how the blood circulates, and the nature of the air we breathe. They have acquired new and almost unlimited powers; they can command the thunders of heaven, mimic the earthquake, and even mock the invisible world with its own shadows.
> 
> 
- A rejection of Frankenstein’s ideals about the nature of science, grounding it in reality and incremental progress rather than mysticism. It will be interesting to see how Frankenstein responds to this idea, and if he adopts it, how it eventually breaks away from him.
> [!PDF|yellow] [[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=98&selection=14,23,16,74&color=yellow|Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus, p.98]]
> > After days and nights of incredible labour and fatigue, I succeeded in discovering the cause of generation and life; nay, more, I became myself capable of bestowing animation upon lifeless matter.
> 
- This is most likely an early turning point in the book where Frankenstein learns about the secret to life. It is this ability that he eventually uses to construct his monster.
> [!PDF|yellow] [[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=99&selection=2,18,5,57&color=yellow|Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus, p.99]]
> >  Learn from me, if not by my precepts, at least by my example, how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge and how much happier that man is who believes his native town to be the world, than he who aspires to become greater than his nature will allow.
> 
- “Ignorance is bliss.” The unintentional consequences of knowledge. Explores how an endless search for knowledge may have terrible results.
> [!PDF|yellow] [[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=100&selection=2,16,5,19&color=yellow|Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus, p.100]]
> > Pursuing these reflections, I thought, that if I could bestow animation upon lifeless matter, I might in process of time (although I now found it impossible) renew life where death had apparently devoted the body to corruption.
> 
- Perhaps part of the reason that Frankenstein wants to learn the secret of life so much was the death of his mother right before he left. This event no doubt left something in him, a desire to learn how to create life, so that he could potentially revive his mother.
> [!PDF|yellow] [[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=103&selection=24,41,27,50&color=yellow|Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus, p.103]]
> > For this I had deprived myself of rest and health. I had desired it with an ardour that far exceeded moderation; but now that I had finished, the beauty of the dream vanished, and breathless horror and disgust filled my heart.
> 
- While for the longest time, Victor had sought to create life, the moment he creates it, he realizes how terrible it had been for him to actually do this.
> ([[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=147&selection=18,43,20,28&color=yellow|Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus, p.147]])
> Shall I not then hate them who abhor me? I will keep no terms with my enemies. I am miserable, and they shall share my wretchedness.
- This is much like Satan’s attitude after being kicked out from heaven, the idea that if one is not able to get what they want, they will cause others to suffer as well.
> ([[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=148&selection=23,49,25,55&color=yellow|Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus, p.148]])
> For the first time, also, I felt what the duties of a creator towards his creature were, and that I ought to render him happy before I complained of his wickedness.
- This is the first time in the book when Frankenstein has finally confronted his responsibility as the creator of his creature, feeling, if only in the slightest amount, some compassion for the plight that he has experienced.
[[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=169&selection=18,65,21,43&color=yellow|The injustice of his sentence was very flagrant; all Paris was indignant; and it was judged that his religion and wealth rather than the crime alleged against him had been the cause of his condemnation.]]
- There much hypocrisy in this statement, as the people were outraged that an Ottoman was judged for his religion and wealth, but when they meet the creature, they judge him the same way.
[[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=261&selection=15,49,18,12|Farewell, Walton! Seek happiness in tranquillity, and avoid ambition, even if it be only the apparently innocent one of distinguishing yourself in science and discoveries.]]
- Victory’s final farewell to Walton, warning him of the dangers of attempting scientific progress. This is likewise a rejection of Shelley of the Enlightenment idea of inevitable human progress and rationalism. Yet, the brutality and unreasonableness of the creature likewise presents itself as a critique of Romanticism.
[[Frankenstein, or, The modern Prometheus.pdf#page=264&selection=24,25,26,41|But it is even so; the fallen angel becomes a malignant devil. Yet even that enemy of God and man had friends and associates in his desolation; I am alone.]]
- The creature expresses his disappointment that he was alone, comparing himself with Satan from *Paradise Lost*. He attributes this to the idea that he was mistreated due to the way he looked.
