## Summary
Polybius’ Histories is a 40-book work that explains how Rome rose from a regional power to dominate the Mediterranean in little more than a century, especially through the aftermath of the Second Punic War and the defeat of Macedon and Carthage. Polybius argues that this success was not accidental but came from Rome’s mixed constitution, military discipline, political institutions, and ability to absorb and adapt to change. He writes as a Greek statesman and hostage living in Rome, so the work also serves as a practical guide to understanding power, causation, and the interconnected nature of world events, with a strong emphasis on eyewitness inquiry, political realism, and lessons for future leaders.
## Significance
This is one of the main sources for historians with regards to the rise of the Roman Republic.

### Political & Military Insights
Polybius argues that political systems follow this cycle:
1. People follow the strongest leader, and one leader eventually starts a **kingship** where they are virtuous. 
2. The kingship deteriorates into a **tyranny** and the people overthrow it.
3. The people establish an **aristocracy**, which is likewise corrupted and becomes an **oligarchy**.
4. People overthrow the oligarchy and establish a **democracy**.
5. The democracy eventually deteriorates into anarchy, and the cycle repeats.

## Tactics Compendium
- The use both a contravallation and circumvallation in the Siege of Agrigentum by the Romans, a tactic that would later by employed by Caesar during the Gallic Wars (specifically the Siege of Alesia).
- The use of the *corvus*, a long, hinged stick with a metal hook at the end, which would have been used against Carthaginian vessels to pull them closer to the Roman vessels and thus neutralize the power of their fleet.
- Hannibal’s crossing of the Alps and the Arno Marshes, which allowed him to penetrate deep into Roman territory without encountering much resistance.
- At the Battle of the Trebia, Hannibal ensures that when the time came for the armies to engage, while the Romans had to ford a river and were left without eating breakfast, the Carthaginians were well-prepared.
- At the Battle of Cannae, the use of a convex line which drew the Romans in and eventually produced a concave shape that allowed for the envelopment of the Roman army.
- At the Battle of Ager Falernus, although Hannibal was trapped in the Capuan valleys, he was able to use a herd of oxen with torches attached to their horns in order to distract the Romans and escape.
