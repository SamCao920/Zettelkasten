## Summary
An essay that describes in broad strokes the arguments and positions relating to the concept of meritocracy.
## Significance
This is crucial for understanding the broad topics and arguments surrounding the idea of meritocracy.
## Historical Survey
Meritocracy has a long tradition in East Asia, mainly due to the perceived consequentialist benefits.
*   “People will be more virtuous, and better cared-for, if they are ruled by the meritorious.” (Mulligan, 2023)Other thinkers, such as Plato and Aristotle have also participated in the tradition of favoring meritocracy.However, while Plato and Eastern thinkers mainly concerned themselves with meritocracy when it comes to governance, A. deals generally with the distribution of social goods.
*   For A. specifically, he believes in proportional equality, meaning that the distribution of social goods is proportional to the merit of the persons.
*   He defines merit roughly as how well something to achieves their intended goal.This idea is also related to the idea of the American Dream.
## Merit
Merit is contextual, i.e., whether someone is meritorious depends on the context which defines their merit. Merit is internal, and is distinct from the outcomes which result from the merit.
*   We can’t judge someone’s merit merely based on outcomes.We must choose between two definitions of merit:
*   **Consequentialist**: one chooses meritocracy because of the results it produces; it is only contingently valuable.
    *   But the issue is that there might be things that are not related to merit that might produce a better outcome.
*   \*\*Categorical: \*\*one chooses meritocracy because running a system based on merit is inherently good.
    *   But the issue then is that you are not always able to produce the best results.
## Merit and Desert
There is no generally agreed upon definition for the two terms. Desert generally is pre-institutional; it is what people deserve that decides institutions/the rules that govern society. It is a concept that can be used to judge institutions to see of they are fair/good or not.
*   A libertarian view would argue that this does not matter, and as long as individuals chose to participate in the institutions, they must abide by the institutions and external considerations of desert are ignored.Desert is also often thought of as having two properties:
*   Aboutness: the basis for desert must be “about” the subject of a desert-claim. This is often construed as responsibility.
*   Fitness: there is a natural connection between the basis for desert and the object being awarded.
## Equal Opportunity
There is two conceptions of equal opportunity:
*   Fair/substantive: people start with the same amount of resources (in the broadest sense of the term) in the initial position.
*   Formal: the institutions in society apply equally and fairly to all.A meritocracy is merely a society where there is equality of opportunity in both senses.There are two salient debates when it comes to equal opportunity that is relevant here:
1.  Whether there is a moral difference between social advantages (being born into a rich family) and natural advantages (being born intelligence).
2.  The extent to which the state can interfere with family affairs to ensure equal opportunity.“The philosophical issues here are to explain what is problematic and what is not, and to draw the line between the government’s interest in establishing equal opportunity and the liberty of parents to raise their children as they see fit.” (Mulligan, 2023)
## Governance
There are three questions principally related to the idea of governing with a meritocracy:
1.  How do you define merit?
2.  How should the meritocrats rule?
3.  Is meritocracy justifiable?
### New Confucian Meritocracy
These theories are based on a mixture of Western liberal political theory and traditional Chinese Confucian political theory.
### Restricted Suffrage
Another set of theories that are broadly related to the concept of restricting the right to make political decisions to those who are sufficiently competent.
### Plural Voting
A set of theories which argues that people should be given differing amounts of votes based on their competence. This has consequences, such as the fact that:
1.  Some people with lower allocations of votes would de facto be restricted from suffrage.
2.  There is no unique assignment of votes (all votes could be multiplied by 2, and the result would be the same).
## Justice
There is obviously an instrumental connection between meritocracy and justice, since many systems of thought see most of the time that putting the most competent in positions of power is the best means of achieving some end. However, the most vocal proponents of meritocracy claim that meritocracy works as a deontological principle as well. One response is to say that since in a meritocracy, people get what they deserve, and getting what one deserves is just, that meritocracy is just. Additionally, meritocracy is intuitively appealing for people, and it is grounded in autonomy, meaning that we consider only the relevant facts (whether someone is able to perform some tasks well) and thus we treat them as agents whose purposeful acts can make a difference in the world (we respect them). Another idea is the the very nature of social goods points to merit based distribution.
*   When the goal is to produce bagels, it is inherently (intuitively) true that the person who should get this job is the person who is best at making bagels. The fact that the person who is best at making bagels gets the job is “primitive” and arises as aesthetic experience does.
### Distribution of Jobs
When it comes to a just distribution of jobs, the meritocrat would argue that only merit be considered, and all non-merit factors ought not to be considered. However, it is difficult to ascertain what exactly constitutes a merit factor.
*   For instance, does beauty count as a merit factor? If a customer is more likely to buy from a sales agent who is beautiful, should that be a factor of consideration in the hiring process?Affirmative action is a policy that is disliked by meritocrats because it violates the principle of equal opportunity, and is discriminatory.
### Distribution of Income
There are a few arguments made in favor of a meritocratic distribution of income:
*   Desert; people who work harder deserve more income.There are multiple ways of measuring economic contribution: WTP, consumer surplus, marginal product.A complication is how to disentangle the contribution of labor vs. capital in the production of something. And it seems that how the capital was acquired is of moral consideration as well.Economic rents is also a problem, since rents are incomes not associated with any sort of economic contribution. For instance:
*   Executive self-dealing
*   Luck-based income
*   Rent-seeking behavior (capturing value without creating it)
*   The beauty premium
## Objections
### Rawls
Rawls claims that meritocracy is only contingently and instrumentally useful in the case that it furthers the Difference Principle. More specifically, he has four critiques of meritocracy:
1.  Targeting desert-based theories broadly, he claims that no one “deserves” anything, since all differences between people are either due to social circumstances or natural differences in ability, neither of which is morally relevant.
2.  He views it as being impossible to accurately track people’s merit and thus distribute things on a basis of merit.
3.  He thinks that it is possible that meritocracy is the result of comprehensive doctrines, and not the result of bargaining between people. Therefore, it would lack universal justification to serve as a shared political base.’
4.  It is insufficiently democratic.
### Relational Egalitarianism
Believes that the fundamental principle of society is equality, and since meritocracy violates that principle, it is morally repugnant.
### Democratic Theory
The first group in this camp that rejects meritocracy are the *epistemic democrats*, who argue that democracy produces good decisions. For instance, they use Condorcet’s Jury Theorem to argue the point of the "wisdom of the crowd." The meritocrat can respond in the following three manners:
*   Deny the relevance of the model, since its assumptions are violated in the real world (no independence condition met, or reject that assumption that the average  $p > 0.5$  ).
*   Concede that the theorem is true, but that meritocracy does better, since it gains all the benefits of the theory (from aggregation), but suffers none of the downsides.
*   Argue that this supports meritocracy, since if one where to give the more informed more votes, the limit of the aggregate probability of the right decisions increases as the amount of voters approaches infinity is faster than in the democratic case.
$$
\prod_{n=0}^{\infty} p_{n} \ \text{where} \ p \in [0,1] \text{ and }p_{n} \text{ is more informed than} \ p_{n+1} 
$$
```
The above approaches 1 faster than merely:
```
$$
\prod_{n=0}^{\infty} p_{n} \ \text{where} \ p \in [0,1]
$$
```
Assuming that $\frac{\sum p_{n}}{n} > 0.5$ in both cases.
```
For *procedural democrats* all that matters is that the principles associated with democracy are carried out, and whether the end effect is better or worse does not matter. *Epistemic proceduralism* attempts to marry the two schools of thought into one.
### Libertarianism
The meritocratic distributive rule of hiring on the basis of merit violates the libertarian right to contract. Additionally, Hayek gives the critique that a meritocratic economy may not be the most efficient, since letting people choose what they want to do maximizes surplus, while choosing based on merit might not.
